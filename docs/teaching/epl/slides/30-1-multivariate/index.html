Mean of a random vector
A random vector and its center
\[\bX=\begin{pmatrix}X_1\\X_2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bmu=\E\bX=\begin{pmatrix}\E X_1\\\E X_2\end{pmatrix}\] Each dot is one pair. An observed pair is a deterministic vector $\bx$.

Here $\bmu=(1,1)^T$. The arrow $\bx-\bmu$ is the pair's deviation from the center.

Question: Add a fixed vector $\bb=(1,-3)^T$ to every pair. What happens to the mean, and to the deviation $\bx-\bmu$?

Every point and the mean move by the same amount: $\E[\bX+\bb]=\bmu+\bb=(2,-2)^T$. The deviations $\bx-\bmu$ stay the same.

Covariance matrix of a two-dimensional vector

For $\bX=(X_1,X_2)^T$ with mean $\bmu=(\mu_1,\mu_2)^T$:

\[\mK=\E[(\bX-\bmu)(\bX-\bmu)^T]\]
\[(\bX-\bmu)(\bX-\bmu)^T=\begin{pmatrix}\textcolor{#A0521B}{(X_1-\mu_1)^2}&\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{(X_1-\mu_1)(X_2-\mu_2)}\\\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{(X_2-\mu_2)(X_1-\mu_1)}&\textcolor{#A0521B}{(X_2-\mu_2)^2}\end{pmatrix}\]

Take the expectation entry by entry:

\[\mK=\begin{pmatrix}\textcolor{#A0521B}{\var(X_1)}&\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{\cov(X_1,X_2)}\\\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{\cov(X_2,X_1)}&\textcolor{#A0521B}{\var(X_2)}\end{pmatrix}\]
Variances on the diagonal, covariances off it
The two off-diagonal entries are equal, so $\mK$ is symmetric. In $d$ dimensions, $\mK_{ij}=\cov(X_i,X_j)$ and $\mK_{ii}=\var(X_i)$.
Sign of the covariance
\[\cov(X_1,X_2)=\E[(X_1-\mu_1)(X_2-\mu_2)]\]

The off-diagonal entry averages the product of the two deviations; with $\bmu=\bzero$ that product is $x_1x_2$.

Question: Which quadrants hold more of the points, and what sign will the average product $x_1x_2$ have?

The positive products outweigh the negative ones in this model.

\[\mK=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \rho=\frac{\cov(X_1,X_2)}{\sigma_1\sigma_2}=0.5\]
Covariance records how deviations move together
It requires finite second moments.
The notes call E[XY^T] a correlation matrix: it is an uncentered second moment, distinct from the normalized correlation coefficient rho here. Cov(X,Y)=E[XY^T]-E[X]E[Y]^T. The 400 dots are a sample; their average product is close to, not equal to, 0.5.

Singular covariance matrices

Die counts in 36 rolls: $\var(X_i)=5$ and $\cov(X_i,X_j)=-1$. With $\bone=(1,\ldots,1)^T$,

\[\mK=6\mI-\bone\bone^T.\]

Each row of $\mK$ sums to $5-5=0$, so $\mK\bone=\bzero$.

The sum has zero variance
\[\var\Big(\sum_{i=1}^6X_i\Big)=\bone^T\mK\bone=0\] The total is always 36. A covariance matrix with a zero-variance direction is singular.
Linear transformations of a random vector
Transform every deviation
\[\begin{aligned}\bY&=\mA\bX+\bb\\ \E\bY&=\mA\bmu+\bb\\ \cov(\bY)&=\mA\,\cov(\bX)\,\mA^T\end{aligned}\] Translation changes the center. The linear map stretches, rotates, or projects the deviations.

Start from $\cov(\bX)=\mI$. Stretch by $\sqrt{1.5}$ along $x_2$ and by $\sqrt{0.5}$ along $x_1$.

Rotate by $45^\circ$: $\cov(\bY)=\mA\mA^T$ has the running model's covariance.

For a scalar projection, $\var(\ba^T\bX)=\ba^T\mK\ba\geq0$: covariance matrices are positive semidefinite.

Trace one deviation through both maps. More generally Cov(AX+a,BY+b)=A Cov(X,Y) B^T. These identities do not require Gaussian distributions.

Solution

Euclidean distance does not measure how likely a point is
A and B are equally far from the mean, yet the density at A is $e^{(16-16/3)/2}\approx207$ times that at B. Mahalanobis distance measures in units of the cloud's spread and ranks them correctly.
The multivariate Gaussian density
\[p_{\bX}(\bx)=\underbrace{\textcolor{#A0521B}{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mK|^{1/2}}}}_{\text{normalizes the density}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac12\underbrace{\textcolor{#087F80}{(\bx-\bmu)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)}}_{\Delta^2(\bx,\bmu)}\Big)\]

The ordinary $d$-dimensional density requires $\mK$ positive definite.

The determinant accounts for the cloud's volume.

Density contours are Mahalanobis ellipses
Up to a constant, $\log p_{\bX}(\bx)=-\tfrac12\Delta^2(\bx,\bmu)$: within this Gaussian, larger Mahalanobis distance means lower density, and each ellipse $\Delta=\text{const}$ is a contour of equal density.
Whitening and Mahalanobis distance
Measure in units of the cloud's spread
A whitening matrix $\mW$ has $\mW\mK\mW^T=\mI$: $\bZ=\mW(\bX-\bmu)$ has covariance $\mI$, and $\mK^{-1}=\mW^T\mW$ gives \[\Delta^2(\bx,\bmu)=\|\mW(\bx-\bmu)\|^2=\|\bz\|^2.\]

For the running model, $\mW$ rotates the long axis onto a coordinate axis,

then divides each axis by its standard deviation: the ellipses become circles, with $\|\bz_A\|\approx2.31$ and $\|\bz_B\|=4$.

Whitening turns Mahalanobis distance into length
Mahalanobis distance is invariant under invertible changes of coordinates when the mean and covariance are transformed too.
One choice is W = Lambda^(-1/2) Q^T from the eigendecomposition K = Q Lambda Q^T: rotate into the principal directions, then divide by their standard deviations. Any W with W^T W = K^(-1) works, for example one built from a Cholesky factor.
Gaussian density as a function of Mahalanobis distance

Write the exponent with $\Delta$:

\[p_{\bX}(\bx)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mK|^{1/2}}\exp\Big(-\frac12\Delta^2(\bx,\bmu)\Big)\]

With the whitened $\bz=\mW(\bx-\bmu)$ and $\Delta=\|\bz\|$:

\[p_{\bX}(\bx)=\frac{1}{|\mK|^{1/2}}\,p_{\bZ}(\bz),\qquad\bZ\sim\cN(\bzero,\mI)\]

Running model, $|\mK|=0.75$: $p_{\bX}(A)\approx0.0128$ at $\Delta_A\approx2.31$ and $p_{\bX}(B)\approx6.2\times10^{-5}$ at $\Delta_B=4$, a ratio of $e^{(16-16/3)/2}\approx207$.

The density depends on $\bx$ only through $\Delta$
Points on one Mahalanobis ellipse share a density. The factor $|\mK|^{-1/2}$ rescales the standard Gaussian for the cloud's volume.
The factor |K|^(-1/2) is the Jacobian of the whitening map: |det W| = |K|^(-1/2), because W K W^T = I.

Origin of the term “regression”
Regression to the mean
Galton (1886): children of tall parents are, on average, less tall. He called it “regression towards mediocrity.” It runs in both directions and needs only $|\rho|<1$: \[\frac{\E[S\mid M=m]-177}{7}=\rho\,\frac{m-163}{6}.\]
Regression in least squares
Least squares is older than the word. Yule (1897) fit Galton's lines by least squares, and “regression” came to mean predicting $Y$ from $X$. The line $a+bX$ with least mean squared error has \[b=\frac{\cov(X,Y)}{\var(X)}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}.\]
Every least-squares line regresses toward the mean
In SD units the least-squares slope is $\rho$. For mothers and sons, $b=0.5\cdot\tfrac76=\tfrac7{12}$ cm per cm: the red line on the previous slide.
Legendre published least squares in 1805 and Gauss in 1809, both for orbits from astronomical observations. Galton first called the effect "reversion" (seeds, 1877) and changed to "regression" once he saw it was symmetric and not genetic (Gorroochurn 2016, The American Statistician). Yule, On the Theory of Correlation, JRSS 60 (1897), 812-854. The population least-squares slope Cov/Var holds for any joint distribution; it equals the conditional mean only when that is linear, as for jointly Gaussian pairs. Frequentist view: log-likelihood of a Gaussian sample

Four illustrative samples.

So far the distribution was known, and we predicted unobserved quantities from observed ones.

Question: How do we find the distribution itself?

Suppose we observe the points on the left, iid from $\cN(\btheta,\mK)$ with $\btheta$ and $\mK$ unknown.

Log-likelihood of $n$ iid samples
\[\begin{aligned}\ell(\btheta,\mK)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln p(\bx_i;\btheta,\mK)\\&=\frac n2\ln|\mK^{-1}|-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx_i-\btheta)+c\end{aligned}\] using $|\mK^{-1}|=1/|\mK|$, with $c=-\frac{nd}{2}\ln(2\pi)$.

Question: Which $\btheta$ and $\mK$ make these points most likely?

Frequentist view: ML estimate of the mean

Four illustrative samples.

Maximize over $\btheta$:

\[\ell(\btheta,\mK)=\frac n2\ln|\mK^{-1}|-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx_i-\btheta)+c\]

For symmetric $\mK^{-1}$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial\btheta}(\bx-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx-\btheta)=-2(\bx-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}$.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\btheta}&=\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}=\bzero^T\\ \Rightarrow\quad&\boxed{\hat\btheta_{\mle}=\bar\bx=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\bx_i}\end{aligned}\]
The center does not depend on $\mK$
$\mK^{-1}$ is invertible, so the residuals (dashed) sum to $\bzero$. Here $\bar\bx=\frac14(4,8)^T=(1,2)^T$.
Frequentist view: ML estimate of the covariance

Four illustrative samples.

Substitute $\hat\btheta_{\mle}=\bar\bx$ and maximize over the precision matrix $\Lambda=\mK^{-1}$:

\[\ell(\Lambda)=\frac n2\ln|\Lambda|-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T\Lambda(\bx_i-\bar\bx)+c\]

For symmetric $\Lambda$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda}\,\bx^T\Lambda\bx=\bx\bx^T$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda}\ln|\Lambda|=\Lambda^{-1}$.

\[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\Lambda}=\frac n2\Lambda^{-1}-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T=\mathsf 0\]
\[\boxed{\hat\mK_{\mle}=\hat\Lambda^{-1}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T}\]
The ML covariance averages outer products
Each residual contributes one outer product, and dividing by $n$ averages them.
Differentiating with respect to the precision matrix keeps both terms simple: the log-determinant gives its inverse and the quadratic form gives the outer product. Treating the entries of Lambda as free gives the same stationary point as imposing symmetry. The next slide computes the four outer products for these samples.

Frequentist view: bias of the ML covariance

Residuals are measured from $\bar\bx$, which is fit to the same samples, so they run shorter than deviations from the true $\btheta^*$.

\[\begin{aligned}&\sum_i(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T\\&\quad=\sum_i(\bx_i-\btheta^*)(\bx_i-\btheta^*)^T-n(\bar\bx-\btheta^*)(\bar\bx-\btheta^*)^T\\&\quad\text{has mean }n\mK^*-n\cdot\tfrac1n\mK^*=(n-1)\,\mK^*.\end{aligned}\]

So $\E\big[\hat\mK_{\mle}\big]=\frac{n-1}n\,\mK^*$. Dividing by $n-1$ instead is unbiased:

\[\boxed{\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T}\]

Here $\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\4&6\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&4/3\\4/3&2\end{pmatrix}$: the same shape, a larger ellipse.

If $n\le d$, the sample covariance is necessarily singular: a nonsingular Gaussian fit needs enough independent directions.

Bayesian view: posterior of the mean, known covariance
Unknown mean, known observation covariance $\mK$
\[\cpr{\bTheta\sim\cN(\bmu_0,\mS_0)},\qquad\clk{\bX_i\mid\bTheta\sim\cN(\bTheta,\mK)}\quad\text{conditionally iid}\]
One coordinate (Chapter 3)
\[\cpo{\frac{1}{\tau_1^2}}=\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}},\qquad \cpo{\mu_1}=\cpo{\tau_1^2}\Big(\cpr{\frac{\theta_0}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}}\Big)\]
A vector
\[\cpo{\mS_n^{-1}}=\cpr{\mS_0^{-1}}+\clk{n\mK^{-1}},\qquad \cpo{\hat\btheta_n}=\cpo{\mS_n}\big(\cpr{\mS_0^{-1}\bmu_0}+\clk{n\mK^{-1}\bar\bx}\big)\]

The posterior is $\cpo{\cN(\hat\btheta_n,\mS_n)}$. The data mean has covariance $\mK/n$; its precision is $n\mK^{-1}$.

Learning adds precision matrices
$\mK$ is the spread of one observation, and $\mS_n$ the uncertainty about the mean. This is Chapter 3's Gaussian update, with a separate uncertainty structure for different directions.
Observation covariance K describes individual measurements; Sn describes uncertainty about the mean. Both covariance matrices are positive definite here.
Bayesian view: posterior mean with unequal precisions
Predict the posterior center
\[\cpr{\bmu_0=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \mS_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}}\] \[\clk{\bar\bx=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad \frac{\mK}{n}=\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}}\] Which source is more precise in each coordinate?
\[\cpo{\hat\btheta_n=\begin{pmatrix}0.4\\1.6\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mS_n=0.8\,\mI}\]
Different directions trust different evidence
The prior gets more weight in coordinate 1; the data get more weight in coordinate 2. The posterior center is off the dashed line between the two centers.
The animation adds the data precision gradually, from none to all of it; the dotted path is the posterior center along the way. Precisions per coordinate: 1 + 1/4 = 5/4 and 1/4 + 1 = 5/4.
Bayesian view: the posterior as the sample size grows

Same prior; individual observations have $\mK=\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, and the data mean stays at $\bar\bx=(2,2)^T$ while $n$ grows.

More data moves the posterior to the data
The data precision $n\mK^{-1}$ grows in both coordinates, and coordinate 2, where the data were already more precise, stays closer to $\bar\bx$.
Summary
The mean locates a cloud; covariance describes its deviations and how a linear map transforms them.
Gaussian ellipses turn covariance into geometry. Mahalanobis distance measures deviations relative to that geometry.
Conditioning uses observed coordinates to shift the mean and reduce uncertainty about the remaining coordinates.
Maximum likelihood learns the center and shape from data. Bayesian mean estimation combines prior and data precision.
Exit question
For jointly Gaussian $X_1,X_2$ with $\rho=0$, what changes in the distribution of $X_1$ when we observe $X_2$? Nothing: the two variables are independent, so the conditional equals the marginal.
