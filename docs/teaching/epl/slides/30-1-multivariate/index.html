Covariance matrix of a two-dimensional vector

For $\bX=(X_1,X_2)^T$ with mean $\bmu=(\mu_1,\mu_2)^T$:

\[\mK=\E[(\bX-\bmu)(\bX-\bmu)^T]\]
\[(\bX-\bmu)(\bX-\bmu)^T=\begin{pmatrix}\textcolor{#A0521B}{(X_1-\mu_1)^2}&\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{(X_1-\mu_1)(X_2-\mu_2)}\\\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{(X_2-\mu_2)(X_1-\mu_1)}&\textcolor{#A0521B}{(X_2-\mu_2)^2}\end{pmatrix}\]

Take the expectation entry by entry:

\[\mK=\begin{pmatrix}\textcolor{#A0521B}{\var(X_1)}&\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{\cov(X_1,X_2)}\\\textcolor{#6A3D9A}{\cov(X_2,X_1)}&\textcolor{#A0521B}{\var(X_2)}\end{pmatrix}\]
The two off-diagonal entries are equal, so $\mK$ is symmetric. In $d$ dimensions, $\mK_{ij}=\cov(X_i,X_j)$ and $\mK_{ii}=\var(X_i)$. In one dimension, the variance averages the squared deviation from the mean. For a random vector, the deviation X minus mu is itself a vector, and squaring becomes multiplying it by its own transpose: a column times a row, which gives a matrix. The covariance matrix K is the average of that matrix. In two dimensions, this matrix holds each coordinate's squared deviation on the diagonal and the product of the two deviations off the diagonal. Averaging entry by entry turns the diagonal into the two variances and the off-diagonal entries into the covariance of X one and X two. The diagonal says how much each coordinate varies on its own; the off-diagonal says how the two vary together. The two off-diagonal entries are equal, so K is symmetric. In any dimension, the entry in row i and column j is the covariance of X i and X j, and the diagonal holds the variances.

\[\cov(X_1,X_2)=\E[(X_1-\mu_1)(X_2-\mu_2)]\]

The off-diagonal entry averages the product of the two deviations; with $\bmu=\bzero$ that product is $x_1x_2$.

Covariance of multinomial counts

Example: $X_i$ counts face $i$ in 36 fair, independent rolls, and $\sum_{i=1}^6 X_i=36$.

Question: If one face occurs more often, what happens to the room left for the others? Predict the sign of $\cov(X_i,X_j)$ for $i\ne j$.

Given $X_1=x_1$, the other $36-x_1$ rolls land on faces 2 to 6 with probability $\frac15$ each, so each of those counts averages $\frac{36-x_1}{5}$ (amber line).

\[\htmlClass{dk2}{\var(X_i)=5},\qquad\htmlClass{dk1}{\cov(X_i,X_j)=-1}\] for $i\ne j$. More of one face leaves fewer rolls for the others.

For each value of $X_1$, one illustrative draw of the other five counts.

Write each count as a sum over the rolls: $I_k(i)=1$ if roll $k$ shows face $i$, else 0, so $X_i=\sum_{k=1}^{36}I_k(i)$ and $\E[I_k(i)]=\frac16$.

For $i\ne j$: $\cov(X_i,X_j)=\sum_{k}\sum_{l}\cov(I_k(i),I_l(j))$.

Different rolls ($k\ne l$) are independent, so those terms are 0.

Same roll ($k=l$): one roll cannot show both faces, so $I_k(i)I_k(j)=0$ and $\cov(I_k(i),I_k(j))=0-\frac16\cdot\frac16=-\frac1{36}$.

Only the 36 same-roll terms remain: $\cov(X_i,X_j)=36\cdot\big(-\frac1{36}\big)=-1$. In general, with $n$ rolls and face probabilities $p_i$, it is $-np_ip_j$.

$\var(X_i)$ the same way: only the same-roll terms remain, each $\var(I_k(i))=\frac16\cdot\frac56$, so $\var(X_i)=36\cdot\frac16\cdot\frac56=5$.

Check: the counts always total 36, so

\[0=\var\Big(\sum_{i=1}^6X_i\Big)=6\cdot5+30\,\cov(X_i,X_j),\]

giving $\cov(X_i,X_j)=-1$ again (30 ordered pairs $i\ne j$).

Singular covariance matrices

Die counts in 36 rolls: $\var(X_i)=5$ and $\cov(X_i,X_j)=-1$. With $\bone=(1,\ldots,1)^T$,

\[\mK=6\mI-\bone\bone^T.\]

Each row of $\mK$ sums to $5-5=0$, so $\mK\bone=\bzero$.

\[\var\Big(\sum_{i=1}^6X_i\Big)=0\] The total is always 36. A covariance matrix with a zero-variance direction is singular. Collecting these entries, the covariance matrix of the six counts has five on the diagonal and minus one everywhere else. Compactly, it is six times the identity minus the all-ones matrix, which is the vector of ones times its transpose. Each row of K adds up to zero, one five and five minus ones, so K times the vector of ones is the zero vector. Keep this fact in mind. The variance of the sum of the X i's is 0. The reason is the total of the six counts is always thirty-six, so it has no variance at all.

Linear transformations of a random vector

\[\begin{aligned}\bY&=\mA\bX+\bb\\ \E\bY&=\htmlClass{dk1}{\mA\bmu+\bb}\\ \cov(\bY)&=\htmlClass{dk1}{\mA\,\cov(\bX)\,\mA^T}\end{aligned}\] Translation changes the center. The linear map stretches, rotates, or projects the deviations.

Start from $\cov(\bX)=\mI$. Stretch by $\sqrt{1.5}$ along $x_1$ and by $\sqrt{0.5}$ along $x_2$.

Rotate by $45^\circ$: $\cov(\bY)=\mA\mA^T$ has the running model's covariance.

Expectation is linear, so $\E\bY=\mA\,\E\bX+\bb=\mA\bmu+\bb$. The shift cancels in the deviation, $\bY-\E\bY=\mA(\bX-\bmu)$, so

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{\cov(\bY)=\E\big[\mA(\bX-\bmu)(\bX-\bmu)^T\mA^T\big]=\mA\,\cov(\bX)\,\mA^T.}\]

In $(x_1,x_2)$ order, stretch by $\mathsf D=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{1.5},\sqrt{0.5})$, then rotate by $\mathsf R$. With $\cov(\bX)=\mI$, $\cov(\bY)=\mA\mA^T$:

\[\htmlClass{dk2}{\begin{aligned}\mA=\mathsf R\mathsf D&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1.5}&0\\0&\sqrt{0.5}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\sqrt3}2&-\frac12\\[2pt]\frac{\sqrt3}2&\frac12\end{pmatrix}\\ \mA\mA^T&=\begin{pmatrix}\frac34+\frac14&\frac34-\frac14\\[2pt]\frac34-\frac14&\frac34+\frac14\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}}\] Trace one deviation through both maps. More generally Cov(AX+a,BY+b)=A Cov(X,Y) B^T. These identities do not require Gaussian distributions. Many quantities of interest are linear functions of a random vector: sums, differences, weighted averages. For Y equal to A X plus b, the mean goes through the same map. The covariance picks up A on the left and A transposed on the right, the matrix counterpart of a constant's square multiplying a variance, and the shift drops out because it leaves the deviations unchanged. On the right is a round cloud with identity covariance. Stretching by the square root of one and a half along x one and by the square root of one half along x two makes the variances one and a half and one half, and the circles become ellipses along the axes. Rotating by forty-five degrees tilts the ellipses along the diagonal. The covariance now has ones on the diagonal and one half off it: our running model is a round cloud that has been stretched and then rotated.

Variance of a linear combination

With $\mA=\ba^T$, $Y=\ba^T\bX=\sum_i a_iX_i$ is one number: \[\E Y=\ba^T\bmu,\qquad \var(Y)=\ba^T\mK\ba.\]

With $\ba=\bone$, $Y$ is the total. For the die counts, $\mK\bone=\bzero$ (previous slide), so

\[\var\Big(\sum_{i=1}^6X_i\Big)=\bone^T\mK\bone=\bone^T\bzero=0.\]
A variance is never negative, so $\ba^T\mK\ba\geq0$ for every $\ba$. 1^T K 1 is the sum of all entries of K. Part 2 uses a^T K a as the variance of a projection. When A is a single row, a transposed, the result is one number, a weighted sum of the coordinates. Its mean is a transposed mu and its variance is a transposed K a: the rule for A K A transposed with a single row, which collapses to a number. Take a equal to the vector of ones, so the weighted sum is the total of the six die counts. On the previous slide we found that K times the vector of ones is zero, so one transposed K one is zero too. The formula confirms that the total, which is always thirty-six, has no variance. A variance is never negative, so a transposed K a is at least zero for every a: every covariance matrix is positive semidefinite. For the die counts it reaches zero at the vector of ones, which is why that K is singular.

From Gaussian RVs to jointly Gaussian vectors

One dimension, $X\sim\cN(\mu,\sigma^2)$:

\[p_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\,e^{-z^2/2},\qquad z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\]

$|z|$ is the distance from the mean in standard deviations, and the density falls exponentially in $z^2$.

Several dimensions: the Mahalanobis distance

\[\Delta(\bx,\bmu)=\sqrt{(\bx-\bmu)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)}\]

$\mK$ controls the geometry of the distance: the shape and tilt of its ellipses.

 We now turn to jointly Gaussian random vectors. Like the one-dimensional Gaussian, they show up in a wide range of applications, especially wherever many small effects add up. To see what they look like, start with a single Gaussian random variable X, with mean mu and standard deviation sigma. Its density, on the right, peaks at the mean and falls off on both sides. What matters is z, the distance from the mean divided by sigma. So distance is measured in standard deviations: a point two units from the mean is far out when sigma is one half, and close in when sigma is ten. The density depends on x only through z, and it falls off exponentially in z squared. That is why it drops so quickly: at one standard deviation it is about sixty percent of the peak, at two about fourteen percent, and at three only about one percent. Now move to two or more dimensions. We still measure distance from the mean in units of the spread, but the spread now depends on the direction: this cloud stretches out along the diagonal and is squeezed across it. The Mahalanobis distance on the slide handles this: it uses the inverse of the covariance matrix K to scale each direction by its own spread. The covariance matrix K controls the geometry of this distance. The curves on the right are the points where Delta equals one, two, and three, and K sets their shape and tilt: they are ellipses, longest in the direction where the data spread most. Let's see how this distance behaves for different covariance matrices K.

Mahalanobis distance for a diagonal covariance

Diagonal covariance, uncorrelated coordinates:

\[\mK=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2&0\\0&\sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mK^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sigma_1^2&0\\0&1/\sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}\]
\[\begin{aligned}\Delta^2&=(\bx-\bmu)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)\\&=\frac{(x_1-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2}+\frac{(x_2-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2}\end{aligned}\]
$\sigma_1=1.5$, $\sigma_2=0.5$: the marked point is $1\,\sigma_1$ and $2\,\sigma_2$ from the mean, so $\Delta^2=1^2+2^2=5$. Let's start with the simplest covariance matrix, a diagonal one. A diagonal K means the two coordinates are uncorrelated, and the numbers on its diagonal are their variances. Its inverse is just as simple: it is diagonal too, holding the reciprocals of the variances. In the picture on the right, the cloud is stretched along x one and squeezed along x two, because sigma one is larger than sigma two. Now put this inverse into the definition. Because K inverse is diagonal, the product treats each coordinate separately, and Delta squared becomes a sum of two terms, one per coordinate. Each term is that coordinate's distance from the mean, measured in its own standard deviations and then squared. So for a diagonal K, the Mahalanobis distance is the one-dimensional distance in standard deviations from the first slide, applied to each coordinate and added up. Here is a concrete case. The picture uses sigma one equal to one and a half and sigma two equal to one half, and the ticks on the axes mark one and two standard deviations in each direction. The marked point is one and a half from the mean along x one, which is only one standard deviation, and just one unit along x two, which is already two standard deviations. So Delta squared is one squared plus two squared, which is five. The smaller step, along x two, counts for more, because the data vary much less in that direction. That is the idea behind the Mahalanobis distance: every direction is measured against its own spread.

Mahalanobis distance for a general covariance

Allowing off-diagonal entries in $\mK$ rotates the axes as well.

$\mK$ then defines its own axes, along its eigenvectors $\bu_1,\bu_2$, with variances $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ along them (its eigenvalues). With $y_1,y_2$ the coordinates of $\bx-\bmu$ along them,

\[\Delta^2=\frac{y_1^2}{\lambda_1}+\frac{y_2^2}{\lambda_2}.\]
A diagonal $\mK$ keeps the coordinate axes, and off-diagonal entries rotate them. So far the covariance matrix was diagonal, and the distance simply measured each coordinate against its own standard deviation. What happens when K also has off-diagonal entries, so that the coordinates are correlated? The short answer is that the axes rotate. On the right is a diagonal case, with variances one and a half and one half along the two coordinate axes. Watch what happens on the next step. Turning the picture by forty-five degrees gives our running model, with correlation one half. The covariance matrix now defines its own set of axes, shown as the dashed lines: the long and short axes of the ellipses. They point along the eigenvectors of K, and the variances along them are its eigenvalues, lambda one and lambda two. The distance is computed in these new axes, exactly as in the diagonal case: take the point's coordinates along these axes, measure each against its own standard deviation, square, and add. For the marked point this gives two, before and after the rotation. So the covariance matrix controls the geometry of the distance in two ways. Its eigenvalues set how much each direction is stretched, and its eigenvectors set which way the axes point. A diagonal K keeps the coordinate axes, and off-diagonal entries rotate them.

The multivariate Gaussian density

\[p_{\bX}(\bx)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\htmlClass{dk1}{|\mK|^{1/2}}}\exp\Big(-\frac12\Delta^2(\bx,\bmu)\Big)\]

This is the one-dimensional density with $z^2$ replaced by $\Delta^2$ and $\sigma$ by $|\mK|^{1/2}$, for positive definite $\mK$.

Written in terms of $\mK$,

\[p_{\bX}(\bx)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mK|^{1/2}}\,e^{-\frac12(\bx-\bmu)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx-\bmu)}.\]
Within one Gaussian, a larger Mahalanobis distance means a lower density. Right: the running model, with $|\mK|=0.75$.

Why $|\mK|^{1/2}$ takes the place of $\sigma$: rotating to coordinates along the axes $\mK$ defines, $\by=\mU^T(\bx-\bmu)$ with $\mU=(\bu_1,\dots,\bu_d)$ its orthonormal eigenvectors, changes no volume and gives $\Delta^2=\sum_i y_i^2/\lambda_i$. The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues, $|\mK|=\prod_i\lambda_i$, so the density is a product of one-dimensional Gaussian densities with variances $\lambda_i$:

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\mK|^{1/2}}\,e^{-\Delta^2/2}=\prod_{i=1}^d\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_i}}\,e^{-y_i^2/2\lambda_i}.}\]

Running model: $|\mK|=1\cdot1-0.5^2=0.75=1.5\times0.5$.

 Now that we know how to measure distance, we can write down the density of a jointly Gaussian vector. It has exactly the shape we anticipated: it falls off exponentially in the squared Mahalanobis distance. Compared with the one-dimensional density, the distance in standard deviations has become the Mahalanobis distance, and the standard deviation in front has become the square root of the determinant of K. That determinant measures how spread out the cloud is overall, and the formula needs K to be positive definite, so that its inverse exists. The Derivation button on the toolbar shows where the square root of the determinant comes from. Writing the Mahalanobis distance out in terms of K gives the form you will see in most books. It is the same formula. Because the density depends on x only through Delta, all points at the same Mahalanobis distance share the same density: the contours of equal density are the ellipses we drew earlier. The plot on the right shows the density of the running model as a function of Delta. It falls quickly: at Delta equal to two it is already down to about fourteen percent of its peak. So within one Gaussian, a larger Mahalanobis distance means a lower density.

Uncorrelated versus independent

A diagonal covariance matrix implies independent components. Knowing one coordinate leaves the other's distribution unchanged. $\cov(X,Y)=0$, yet knowing $X$ determines $Y$ exactly.

Regression to the mean
Galton (1886): children of tall parents are, on average, less tall. He called it “regression towards mediocrity.” It runs in both directions and needs only $|\rho|<1$: \[\frac{\E[D\mid M=m]-163}{6}=\rho\,\frac{m-163}{6}.\]
Regression in least squares
Least squares is older than the word. Yule (1897) fit Galton's lines by least squares, and “regression” came to mean predicting $Y$ from $X$. The line $a+bX$ with least mean squared error has \[\htmlClass{dk1}{b=\frac{\cov(X,Y)}{\var(X)}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}}.\]
In SD units the least-squares slope is $\rho$. For mothers and daughters, $b=0.5\cdot\tfrac66=0.5$ cm per cm: the red line on the previous slide.

Minimize $f(a,b)=\E[(Y-a-bX)^2]$, a convex quadratic in $(a,b)$, by setting both partial derivatives to zero:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial f}{\partial a}&=-2\,\E[Y-a-bX]=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad a=\E Y-b\,\E X\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial b}&=-2\,\E[X(Y-a-bX)]=0\end{aligned}\]

Substitute $a$ into the second condition: $\E\big[X\big((Y-\E Y)-b(X-\E X)\big)\big]=0$, that is, $\cov(X,Y)-b\var(X)=0$. So

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{b=\frac{\cov(X,Y)}{\var(X)}=\frac{\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X^2}=\rho\,\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}.}\]

Mothers ($X$) and daughters ($Y$): $\rho=0.5$ and $\sigma_X=\sigma_Y=6$, so $b=0.5$ cm per cm, the slope of $\E[D\mid M=m]$ on the previous slide.

We split the vector into the part to predict, a, and the part we observe, b, and split the mean and the covariance matrix the same way, as on the left. As long as the observed block's covariance is invertible, the conditional distribution is again Gaussian. Its mean starts at the mean of the predicted part and adds a correction proportional to how far the observation is from its own mean, with the factor K a b times K b b inverse playing the role of the slope. Its covariance starts at K a a and subtracts a term that measures how much the observation reduces our uncertainty. With one coordinate in each block, this is our running model: the correction factor is rho, one half, and the conditional variance is one minus rho squared, zero point seven five, the numbers we found at the start of this part. So one update rule handles any number of observed coordinates. The conditional stays Gaussian, and the amount subtracted from the covariance is positive semidefinite, so observing more coordinates can only reduce our uncertainty.
Part 3 done: the map pulls back; the click zooms into part 4, from data to distribution. That completes conditioning. Pulling back to the whole map, three parts are behind us: random vectors and their covariance, the Gaussian random vector, and how observing some coordinates changes what we know about the rest. Every one of those calculations started from a known mean and covariance. The last part asks where they come from when all we have is data, and it answers twice: first with maximum likelihood, then with a Bayesian prior.

Frequentist view: log-likelihood of a Gaussian sample

So far the distribution was known, and we predicted unobserved quantities from observed ones.

Question: How do we find the distribution itself?

Suppose we observe the points on the right, iid from $\cN(\btheta,\mK)$ with $\btheta$ and $\mK$ unknown.

\[\begin{aligned}\ell(\btheta,\mK)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln p(\bx_i;\btheta,\mK)\\&=\frac n2\ln|\mK^{-1}|-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx_i-\btheta)+c\end{aligned}\] using $|\mK^{-1}|=1/|\mK|$, with $c=-\frac{nd}{2}\ln(2\pi)$.

Question: Which $\btheta$ and $\mK$ make these points most likely?

Four illustrative samples.

In practice nobody hands us the mean and the covariance. What we have is a sample, and the task is to go from the data back to the distribution. This is the estimation problem of Chapter 2, now for a whole vector at once: one mean for each coordinate and a full covariance matrix. Here is a small sample to work with, the four green points on the right. We model them as independent draws from one Gaussian, with mean theta and covariance K, and both are unknown. As in Chapter 2, we maximize the log-likelihood, the sum over the points of the log density. For a Gaussian it has two parts that pull against each other. The sum of squared Mahalanobis distances from theta rewards a mean close to the points and a covariance wide enough to cover them. The log-determinant term penalizes a covariance wider than it needs to be; without it, an enormous covariance would make every point look close. So estimation becomes an optimization: which theta and which K make these four points most likely? We find the mean first, then the covariance.

Frequentist view: ML estimate of the mean

Maximize over $\btheta$:

\[\ell(\btheta,\mK)=\frac n2\ln|\mK^{-1}|-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx_i-\btheta)+c\]

For symmetric $\mK^{-1}$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial\btheta}(\bx-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx-\btheta)=-2(\bx-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}$.

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\btheta}&=\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}=\bzero^T\\ \Rightarrow\quad&\boxed{\hat\btheta_{\mle}=\bar\bx=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\bx_i}\end{aligned}\]
$\mK^{-1}$ is invertible, so the residuals (dashed) sum to $\bzero$. Here $\bar\bx=\frac14(8,4)^T=(2,1)^T$.

Four illustrative samples.

Start with the mean. Only the second term depends on theta, so we differentiate it with the rule in small print, the vector version of the derivative of a square. Setting the derivative to zero gives the answer in one line: the maximum likelihood estimate of the mean is x bar, the plain average of the data points. Notice that K dropped out. The sum of residuals is multiplied by K inverse, which is invertible, so the residuals themselves must add up to zero, whatever K is. On the right, the dashed lines are those residuals, from the center x bar to each point. Our four points add up to eight in the first coordinate and four in the second, so x bar is two, one.

Frequentist view: ML estimate of the covariance

Substitute $\hat\btheta_{\mle}=\bar\bx$ and maximize over the precision matrix $\Lambda=\mK^{-1}$:

\[\ell(\Lambda)=\frac n2\ln|\Lambda|-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T\Lambda(\bx_i-\bar\bx)+c\]

For symmetric $\Lambda$: $\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda}\,\bx^T\Lambda\bx=\bx\bx^T$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\Lambda}\ln|\Lambda|=\Lambda^{-1}$.

\[\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\Lambda}=\frac n2\Lambda^{-1}-\frac12\sum_{i}(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T=\mathsf 0\]
\[\htmlClass{dk3}{\boxed{\hat\mK_{\mle}=\hat\Lambda^{-1}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T}}\] Each residual contributes one outer product, and dividing by $n$ averages them.

Four illustrative samples.

Differentiate entry by entry, treating the $\Lambda_{ij}$ as free variables.

$\bx^T\Lambda\bx=\sum_{i,j}x_i\Lambda_{ij}x_j$, so $\partial(\bx^T\Lambda\bx)/\partial\Lambda_{ij}=x_ix_j$, the $(i,j)$ entry of $\bx\bx^T$.

Expand $|\Lambda|$ along row $i$: $|\Lambda|=\sum_j\Lambda_{ij}C_{ij}$, where the cofactor $C_{ij}$ does not involve $\Lambda_{ij}$. So $\partial|\Lambda|/\partial\Lambda_{ij}=C_{ij}$. The inverse is the transposed cofactor matrix over the determinant, so for symmetric $\Lambda$

\[\htmlClass{dk2}{\frac{\partial\ln|\Lambda|}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}=\frac{C_{ij}}{|\Lambda|}=(\Lambda^{-1})_{ji}=(\Lambda^{-1})_{ij}.}\]

Apply the first fact to each residual $\bx_i-\bar\bx$ and set the gradient to $\mathsf 0$:

\[\htmlClass{dk3}{\frac n2\hat\Lambda^{-1}=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T.}\]

Multiply by $2/n$ and use $\hat\mK_{\mle}=\hat\Lambda^{-1}$. The log-determinant is concave and the other term is linear in $\Lambda$, so this stationary point is the maximum.

 Differentiating with respect to the precision matrix keeps both terms simple: the log-determinant gives its inverse and the quadratic form gives the outer product. Treating the entries of Lambda as free gives the same stationary point as imposing symmetry. The next slide computes the four outer products for these samples. Now the covariance. We plug in x bar for the mean and maximize over the covariance. The algebra is cleaner in terms of K inverse, called the precision matrix, lambda: the matrix version of the precision from Chapter 3. In terms of lambda, the log-likelihood has a log-determinant and a term that is linear in lambda, and the two derivative rules in small print handle them. Setting the derivative to zero balances the two terms. The log-determinant contributes lambda inverse, which is K itself, and the quadratic term contributes, for each point, its residual times its own transpose. Solving gives the maximum likelihood covariance: the average over the points of each residual times its own transpose. In one dimension this is the average squared residual; for vectors the square becomes a column times a row, an outer product, so the estimate averages one matrix per point. The ellipse on the right is the fitted Gaussian at Mahalanobis distance one. The steps behind the two derivative rules are under Derivation in the toolbar.

Frequentist view: bias of the ML covariance

Residuals are measured from $\bar\bx$, which is fit to the same samples, so they run shorter than deviations from the true $\btheta^*$.

\[\begin{aligned}&\sum_i(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T\\&\quad=\sum_i(\bx_i-\btheta^*)(\bx_i-\btheta^*)^T-n(\bar\bx-\btheta^*)(\bar\bx-\btheta^*)^T\\&\quad\text{has mean }n\mK^*-n\cdot\tfrac1n\mK^*=(n-1)\,\mK^*.\end{aligned}\]

So $\E\big[\hat\mK_{\mle}\big]=\frac{n-1}n\,\mK^*$. Dividing by $n-1$ instead is unbiased:

\[\boxed{\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(\bx_i-\bar\bx)(\bx_i-\bar\bx)^T}\]

Here $\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}6&4\\4&6\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&4/3\\4/3&2\end{pmatrix}$: the same shape, a larger ellipse.

If $n\le d$, the sample covariance is necessarily singular: a nonsingular Gaussian fit needs enough independent directions.

 The identity follows by writing x_i - xbar = (x_i - theta*) - (xbar - theta*) and expanding; the cross terms sum to -n(xbar - theta*)(xbar - theta*)^T. Cov(xbar) = K*/n for iid samples. With n = 4 the unbiased estimate is 4/3 times the ML estimate. Is this a good estimate? Chapter 2 asked the same question about the variance of one variable, and the answer carries over. The residuals are measured from x bar, which was fit to these same points, so x bar sits closer to them than the true mean, theta star, does. The residuals run short, and the estimate comes out too small on average. The identity makes this exact. The sum of outer products around x bar equals the sum around the true mean, minus n times the outer product of x bar's own error. On average, the first part is n copies of the true covariance, and the second takes away one copy, because x bar has covariance K over n. That leaves n minus one copies. So dividing by n gives n minus one over n times the true covariance, a little too small, just as for the variance in Chapter 2. Dividing by n minus one instead gives an unbiased estimate. With our four points we divide by three instead of four, so the matrix grows by four thirds: two on the diagonal and four thirds off it. On the right, the dashed ellipse has the same shape and tilt, only larger. One caution. With no more samples than dimensions, the residuals cannot point in every direction, because they add up to zero. The estimated covariance is then singular: some direction gets zero variance, as with the die counts in the first part, and the density formula, which needs K inverse, no longer applies.

Bayesian view: posterior of the mean, known covariance

\[\cpr{\bTheta\sim\cN(\bmu_0,\mS_0)},\qquad\clk{\bX_i\mid\bTheta\sim\cN(\bTheta,\mK)}\quad\text{conditionally iid}\]
One coordinate (Chapter 3)
\[\cpo{\frac{1}{\tau_1^2}}=\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}},\qquad \cpo{\mu_1}=\cpo{\tau_1^2}\Big(\cpr{\frac{\theta_0}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}}\Big)\]
A vector
\[\htmlClass{dk1}{\cpo{\mS_n^{-1}}=\cpr{\mS_0^{-1}}+\clk{n\mK^{-1}}},\qquad \htmlClass{dk2}{\cpo{\hat\btheta_n}=\cpo{\mS_n}\big(\cpr{\mS_0^{-1}\bmu_0}+\clk{n\mK^{-1}\bar\bx}\big)}\]

The posterior is $\cpo{\cN(\hat\btheta_n,\mS_n)}$. The data mean has covariance $\mK/n$; its precision is $n\mK^{-1}$.

$\mK$ is the spread of one observation, and $\mS_n$ the uncertainty about the mean. This is Chapter 3's Gaussian update, with a separate uncertainty structure for different directions.

Log prior plus log likelihood, up to constants; then expand with $(\ba-\bb)^T\mA(\ba-\bb)=\ba^T\mA\ba-2\ba^T\mA\bb+\bb^T\mA\bb$ for symmetric $\mA$, use $\sum_i\bx_i=n\bar\bx$, and collect powers of $\btheta$:

\[\begin{aligned}\ln p(\btheta\mid\bx_1^n)&=-\tfrac12(\btheta-\bmu_0)^T\mS_0^{-1}(\btheta-\bmu_0)\\ &\quad-\tfrac12\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n(\bx_i-\btheta)^T\mK^{-1}(\bx_i-\btheta)+c\\ &=-\tfrac12\,\btheta^T\htmlClass{dk1}{(\mS_0^{-1}+n\mK^{-1})}\,\btheta+\btheta^T\htmlClass{dk2}{(\mS_0^{-1}\bmu_0+n\mK^{-1}\bar\bx)}+c'.\end{aligned}\]

A Gaussian $\cN(\boldsymbol m,\mS)$ has $\ln p(\btheta)=-\tfrac12\,\btheta^T\mS^{-1}\btheta+\btheta^T\mS^{-1}\boldsymbol m+c''$. Matching the two:

Quadratic terms: $\mS_n^{-1}=\mS_0^{-1}+n\mK^{-1}$.

Linear terms: $\mS_n^{-1}\hat\btheta_n=\mS_0^{-1}\bmu_0+n\mK^{-1}\bar\bx$. Multiply by $\mS_n$.

Given $\bTheta$, the $\bX_i$ are independent with covariance $\mK$, so $\cov(\bar\bX\mid\bTheta)=\frac{1}{n^2}\,n\mK=\mK/n$.

 Observation covariance K describes individual measurements; Sn describes uncertainty about the mean. Both covariance matrices are positive definite here. Now the Bayesian view. The unknown mean is a random vector, theta, with a Gaussian prior, in blue, and given theta, the observations are independent Gaussians around it with a known covariance K, in green. On the left is the one-coordinate update from Chapter 3: precisions add, and the posterior mean weights the prior mean and the data mean by their precisions. On the right is the same update for a vector. The data enter through x bar, whose covariance is K over n, so its precision is n times K inverse. So learning adds precision matrices. Keep the two covariances apart: K is the spread of a single observation, while S n is our remaining uncertainty about the mean, and it shrinks as data arrive. Because these are matrices, the update can trust the data more in some directions than in others, as the next example shows.

Summary

The mean locates a cloud; covariance describes its deviations and how a linear map transforms them.
Gaussian ellipses turn covariance into geometry. Mahalanobis distance measures deviations relative to that geometry.
Conditioning uses observed coordinates to shift the mean and reduce uncertainty about the remaining coordinates.
Maximum likelihood learns the center and shape from data. Bayesian mean estimation combines prior and data precision.
For jointly Gaussian $X_1,X_2$ with $\rho=0$, what changes in the distribution of $X_2$ when we observe $X_1$? Nothing: the two variables are independent, so the conditional equals the marginal.

To pull the chapter together: the mean locates a cloud of points, and the covariance measures its spread and tilt; both follow simple rules under linear maps.
