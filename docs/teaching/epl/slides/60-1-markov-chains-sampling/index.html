From Graphical Models to Markov Chains

A Markov chain (MC) is a stochastic process whose future is independent from its past and can be represented as the following Bayesian network:

\[p(x_{t+1}\mid x_0,\ldots,x_t)=p(x_{t+1}\mid x_t)\]

Given the present, the past provides no additional information about the next state.

States and State Space

State: value $x_t$ at time $t$.
State space: all possible states.

Characterizing a Chain

Initial distribution: $p(x_0)$

Transition probabilities: $P_{ij}=p(x_{t+1}=j\mid x_t=i)$

Time-homogeneous: $P_{ij}$ is independent of $t$ (the same for all time instances).

Detailed Balance

Suppose 700 patients start in the stationary distribution: 500 in remission and 200 in relapse.

Divide by 700 to get probability flows

\[\frac{500}{700}\cdot0.2=\frac{200}{700}\cdot0.5=\frac17.\]
\[\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}\qquad\text{for all }i,j.\]

Stationarity: total probability flow in equals total flow out at each state.

Detailed balance: probability flows balance between every pair of states.

3 min. Begin with 500 patients in remission and 200 in relapse. Each dot represents 10 patients. Reveal the expected one-step flows: 20 percent of 500 gives 100 expected remission-to-relapse transitions, while 50 percent of 200 gives 100 expected relapse-to-remission transitions. These are expectations, not guaranteed counts in a random cohort. Thus remission has 400 expected stays plus 100 expected arrivals and relapse has 100 stays plus 100 arrivals. Divide by 700 to turn patient counts into probability flows. The distribution is pi=(5/7,2/7), and each directional flow is 1/7. Transition probabilities differ but the flows agree. Generalize to pi_i P_ij=pi_j P_ji for every pair. Stationarity balances the sum of flows entering and leaving each state, whereas detailed balance balances each pair separately. With more than two states, total flow can balance through circulating flows without detailed balance. The next slide proves that pairwise balance implies stationarity.

Why Detailed Balance Works

For a regular Markov chain, a probability distribution $\boldsymbol\pi$ satisfying detailed balance is the unique stationary distribution: $\boldsymbol\pi=\boldsymbol\sigma$.

Sum the flows into state $j$

\[(\boldsymbol\pi P)_j=\sum_i\textcolor{#2354A8}{\pi_iP_{ij}}\]
\[=\sum_i\textcolor{#CC8800}{\pi_jP_{ji}}\]

Detailed balance replaces each flow by its reverse.

\[=\pi_j\underbrace{\sum_iP_{ji}}_{\text{row }j\text{ sums to }1}=\pi_j.\]

Hence $\boldsymbol\pi P=\boldsymbol\pi$.

Two-state example

\[\pi_1\alpha=\pi_2\beta,\qquad\pi_1+\pi_2=1.\]
\[(\alpha+\beta)\pi_1=\beta\] \[\boxed{\boldsymbol\sigma=\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\right)}\]

For $\alpha+\beta>0$.

Can we design transitions that satisfy detailed balance for a desired target distribution?

2 min. State the theorem with its regularity assumption and uniqueness conclusion. The proof of stationarity itself uses only detailed balance and the fact that each row of P sums to one. For fixed j, sum incoming flows, replace each by its equal reverse flow, and factor out pi_j. The sum over i now runs across row j, so it equals one. Regularity supplies uniqueness and convergence from any initial distribution through the previous theorem. For the two-state exercise, probabilities from state 1 to 2 and back are alpha and beta, with self-loops 1-alpha and 1-beta. DBP gives pi_1 alpha=pi_2 beta. Substitute pi_2=1-pi_1 to get (alpha+beta)pi_1=beta, then the displayed stationary distribution. The formula is valid for alpha+beta>0. If both are zero, every distribution is stationary. If both equal one, the formula still gives a stationary distribution although point starts oscillate, so do not apply the regular-chain convergence guarantee there. End with the design question to motivate constructing MCMC transitions for a chosen target.

Why Sampling?

\[p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal D)=\frac{p(\boldsymbol\theta)p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)}{p(\mathcal D)}=\frac{q(\boldsymbol\theta)}{Z_q},\qquad Z_q=\int q(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta.\]

Two computational difficulties

  1. Unknown normalizer. We can evaluate $q$, but computing $Z_q=p(\mathcal D)$ may be infeasible.
  2. Intractable expectations. Even with $p$, we still need integrals such as \[\E_p[h(\boldsymbol\Theta)]=\int h(\boldsymbol\theta)p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal D)\,d\boldsymbol\theta.\]

For independent draws $\boldsymbol\theta_i\sim p$,

\[\E_p[h(\boldsymbol\Theta)]\approx\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N h(\boldsymbol\theta_i).\]
Samples $N$

Illustration: $p(\theta)=12\theta^2(1-\theta)$ on $[0,1]$

Posterior mean: $h(\boldsymbol\theta)=\boldsymbol\theta$
Event probability: $h(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf1\{\boldsymbol\theta\in A\}$

Two obstacles motivate sampling. Bayes' rule gives q(theta)=p(theta)p(D|theta), with evidence Z_q=p(D) for a proper prior. We may evaluate q pointwise without being able to normalize it. Even a normalized posterior may lead to an intractable integral for an expectation. Posterior means and probabilities of events are both expectations. The law of large numbers motivates the Monte Carlo average with its required factor 1/N. The plot uses independent Beta(3,2) draws, with exact mean 3/5, as an illustration where sampling is already easy. This does not itself solve the difficult-posterior sampling problem. In Bayesian regression, the same principle lets us draw a variance, then regression parameters conditional on that variance, and finally a predictive outcome instead of integrating a complicated marginal posterior. In the following slides E_q means expectation under p=q/Z_q, consistent with the notes' convention for an unnormalized shape.

For a continuous, invertible CDF $F$:

  1. Draw $U\sim\operatorname{Uniform}(0,1)$.
  2. Return $\Theta=F^{-1}(U)$.
\[\Pr(\Theta\le c)=\Pr(U\le F(c))=F(c).\]

Support: $0\le\theta\le1$.

\[F(\theta)=\int_0^\theta 2t\,dt=\theta^2\] \[\Longrightarrow\quad\Theta=F^{-1}(U)=\sqrt U.\]

Why Rejection Sampling Works

Let $A$ mean acceptance, $Z_q=\int q(\theta)\,d\theta$, and $Z_g=\int g(\theta)\,d\theta$.

Proposal density $r=g/Z_g$, with $q\le Mg$.

\[p(\theta',A)=\frac{g(\theta')}{Z_g}\frac{q(\theta')}{Mg(\theta')}=\frac{q(\theta')}{MZ_g}.\]
\[p(A)=\int p(\theta',A)\,d\theta'=\frac{Z_q}{MZ_g}.\]
\[p(\theta'\mid A)=\frac{p(\theta',A)}{p(A)}=\frac{q(\theta')}{Z_q}.\]

Accepted samples have the target distribution.

If $Z_q=Z_g=1$, the acceptance rate is $1/M$.

Self-evaluating: the rejection rate reveals wasted proposals. A loose envelope can be very inefficient.

Proposals $N$

Beta example: $Z_q=1/12$, $Z_g=1/2$, $M=1/4$, so $p(A)=2/3$.

Compute the joint density of a proposal and acceptance first. The g terms cancel, leaving q divided by M Z_g. Integrate to obtain P(A)=Z_q/(M Z_g). Conditioning on A divides the joint density by this acceptance probability, leaving q/Z_q. Thus accepted draws follow the target even when both q and g are unnormalized. In the Beta example the exact rate is (1/12)/((1/4)(1/2))=2/3. The green histogram uses only accepted samples and is normalized by the number accepted and the bin width, while the purple curve is the normalized density 12 theta squared (1-theta). The independent uniform streams and seed match the preceding rejection demonstration. The slider counts all proposals, including rejects. Rejection sampling exposes its efficiency through the observed acceptance fraction. This proof can be studied independently if class time is short.

Why Importance Sampling Works

Let $p=q/Z_q$, $r=g/Z_g$, and $w(\boldsymbol\theta)=q(\boldsymbol\theta)/g(\boldsymbol\theta)$. Assume $g>0$ wherever $q>0$. Draw $\boldsymbol\theta_i$ independently from $r$.

\[\E_q[h(\boldsymbol\Theta)]=\frac{\int h(\boldsymbol\theta)q(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta}{\int q(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta}=\frac{\int h(\boldsymbol\theta)[q(\boldsymbol\theta)/g(\boldsymbol\theta)]g(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta}{\int [q(\boldsymbol\theta)/g(\boldsymbol\theta)]g(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d\boldsymbol\theta}\]
\[=\frac{Z_g\E_r[h(\boldsymbol\Theta)w(\boldsymbol\Theta)]}{Z_g\E_r[w(\boldsymbol\Theta)]}=\frac{\E_r[hw]}{\E_r[w]}\]
\[\approx\frac{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N h(\boldsymbol\theta_i)w_i}{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N w_i}=\boxed{\frac{\sum_i w_i h(\boldsymbol\theta_i)}{\sum_i w_i}}\]

Both unknown normalizers cancel. All proposal draws contribute.

With suitable integrability, the ratio converges to the target expectation. It need not be unbiased for finite $N$.

Start from the normalized expectation as a ratio of integrals. Multiply and divide by the proposal shape g wherever q is nonzero. Rewriting integrals using the actual proposal density r=g/Z_g introduces a factor Z_g in numerator and denominator, which cancels. Define w=q/g. Apply the law of large numbers separately to the numerator and denominator expectations, then cancel the 1/N factors. The target's Z_q is eliminated by the original ratio, and a common scale in g also cancels. Require proposal support wherever the relevant target mass lies and finite target expectation, with positive finite normalizers. The estimator is self-normalized and generally biased at finite N, although consistent under the usual conditions. This derivation provides a self-contained reading slide and can be skipped during class if needed.

Metropolis Algorithm

Target distribution to sample from:

$p(x)=q(x)/Z$, $\quad X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$

Acceptance probability

Higher target density: always accept.

Why Metropolis Works

Choosing the jump distribution

Choose an easy-to-sample $J$. It need not have $p$ as its stationary distribution.

\[x'=x+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim U(-1,1).\]

Conditions on the jump distribution

Reachability: the chain must explore every region of positive target probability, possibly over multiple transitions.

A stronger sufficient condition (one step):

\[J(x'\mid x)>0\quad\text{for all }x,x'\in D,\]

where $D=\{x:p(x)>0\}$.

One-step reachability is not necessary.
Our local jumps can explore $\mathbb R$ over several steps.

Symmetry: $J(x'\mid x)=J(x\mid x')$.

This lets the standard Metropolis acceptance rule satisfy detailed balance.

Detailed balance

For $x'\ne x$, $P(x,x')=J(x'\mid x)\alpha(x,x')$.

\[p(x)P(x,x')\]
\[=p(x)J(x'\mid x)\min\left(1,\frac{p(x')}{p(x)}\right)\]
\[=J(x'\mid x)\min\{p(x),p(x')\}\]
\[=\underbrace{J(x\mid x')}_{\text{symmetry}}\min\{p(x'),p(x)\}\]
\[=p(x')P(x',x).\]
Detailed balance guarantees $p$ is stationary.
Reachability and appropriate convergence conditions ensure exploration and convergence to $p$.

Metropolis-Hastings

How can Metropolis-Hastings work when the jump distribution is asymmetric?

The problem: asymmetric $J$ proposes some moves more often than their reverse. Ordinary Metropolis does not correct this imbalance, so detailed balance may fail.

Correct the proposal imbalance

\[\alpha(x,x')=\min\left(1,\frac{q(x')J(x\mid x')}{q(x)J(x'\mid x)}\right).\]

The reverse / forward ratio $J(x\mid x')/J(x'\mid x)$ corrects proposal asymmetry.

Why the correction works

\[p(x)P(x,x')\] \[=\min\{p(x)J(x'\mid x),\;p(x')J(x\mid x')\}\]
\[=p(x')P(x',x).\]

Detailed balance holds without requiring symmetry of $J$.

Symmetric $J$: the proposal ratio is $1$, so we recover the original Metropolis acceptance rule.
