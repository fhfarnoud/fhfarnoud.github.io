Running example: daily travel times $y_1,\dotsc,y_n$ on one route, with $Y_i\mid\Theta=\theta\sim\cN(\theta,\sigma^2)$ and $\sigma^2$ known.
A flat prior on the whole line

Travel times $y_1^n$, independent given $\theta$:

\[\clk{p(y_i\mid\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-(y_i-\theta)^2/(2\sigma^2)}}.\]

With no information about $\theta$, take $\cpr{p(\theta)\propto1}$ on $\bbR$.

Since $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}1\,d\theta=\infty$, this is not a distribution: an improper prior. It is acceptable when the posterior it produces is proper. An improper prior need not be uniform.

Predict: what shape does the posterior have, and where is its centre?

$n=4$, $\bar y=12$, $\sigma^2=4$

The posterior is the likelihood, renormalized in $\theta$
\[\begin{aligned} \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)\propto1},\\ \clk{\text{Likelihood:}}\quad &\clk{p(y_1^n\mid\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)},\\ \cpo{\text{Posterior:}}\quad &\cpo{p(\theta\mid y_1^n)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(\theta^2-2y_i\theta+y_i^2)}{2\sigma^2}\right)}\\ &\cpo{\phantom{p(\theta\mid y_1^n)}\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\theta^2-2\bar y\theta}{2\sigma^2/n}\right)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\bar y)^2}{2\sigma^2/n}\right)}. \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\cpo{\Theta\mid y_1^n\sim\cN(\bar y,\sigma^2/n)}}\]
The posterior mean is the MLE
Completing the square turns the likelihood into a Gaussian in $\theta$ with mean $\bar y=\hat\theta_{\mle}$ and variance $\sigma^2/n$.

Four days of travel times with $\bar y=12$ and $\sigma^2=4$:

Today: $\cpo{\Theta\mid y_1^n\sim\cN(\bar y,\sigma^2/n)}$, the parameter given this one sample.

Same width, different object
Frequentist: the spread of the estimator $\bar Y$ over samples we could have drawn. Bayesian: the spread of $\Theta$ given the sample we have.
Tomorrow's travel time has mean $\bar y$

By iterated expectations, with $Y_{n+1}\independent Y_1^n\mid\Theta$,

\[\E[Y_{n+1}\mid y_1^n]=\E\big[\E[Y_{n+1}\mid\Theta]\,\big|\,y_1^n\big]=\E[\Theta\mid y_1^n]=\bar y.\]
The spread of tomorrow's travel time

The mean of tomorrow's travel time is $\E[Y_{n+1}\mid y_1^n]=\bar y$.

Predict: is tomorrow's travel time more or less spread out than $\Theta$ is?

\[\begin{aligned} \cpd{\var(Y_{n+1}\mid y_1^n)}&=\cml{\sigma^2}+\cpo{\frac{\sigma^2}{n}},\\ \cpd{Y_{n+1}\mid y_1^n}&\cpd{\sim\cN\!\left(\bar y,\,\sigma^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)}. \end{aligned}\]
Two sources of uncertainty, added
$\cml{\sigma^2}$ is the day-to-day randomness of $Y$ and never shrinks; $\cpo{\sigma^2/n}$ is the uncertainty about the mean and vanishes as $n$ grows.
Why the predictive variance is $\sigma^2+\sigma^2/n$

Condition on $\Theta$ first, then average over the posterior:

\[\begin{aligned} \cpd{\var(Y_{n+1}\mid y_1^n)}&=\E\big[(Y_{n+1}-\bar y)^2\,\big|\,y_1^n\big]\\ &=\E\Big[\E\big[(Y_{n+1}-\bar y)^2\,\big|\,\Theta,y_1^n\big]\,\Big|\,y_1^n\Big]\\ &=\E\big[\sigma^2+(\Theta-\bar y)^2\,\big|\,y_1^n\big]\\ &=\cml{\sigma^2}+\cpo{\var(\Theta\mid y_1^n)}=\cml{\sigma^2}+\cpo{\sigma^2/n}. \end{aligned}\]

The third line uses $\E[(Y_{n+1}-\bar y)^2\mid\Theta=\theta]=\sigma^2+(\theta-\bar y)^2$, the variance plus the squared offset of the mean. The predictive distribution is Gaussian, so its mean and variance determine it.

A proper Gaussian prior

Prior $\Theta\sim\cN(\theta_0,\tau_0^2)$, with mean $\theta_0$ and variance $\tau_0^2$:

\[\begin{aligned} \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\theta_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}\right)},\\[2pt] \clk{\text{Likelihood:}}\quad &\clk{p(y_1^n\mid\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}. \end{aligned}\]

The posterior exponent is a quadratic in $\theta$

Prior $\Theta\sim\cN(\theta_0,\tau_0^2)$ and travel times $Y_i\mid\Theta=\theta\sim\cN(\theta,\sigma^2)$:

\[\begin{aligned} \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\theta_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}\right)},\\[4pt] \clk{\text{Likelihood:}}\quad &\clk{p(y_1^n\mid\theta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)},\\[4pt] \cpo{\text{Posterior:}}\quad &\cpo{p(\theta\mid y_1^n)}\propto\clk{\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)}\times\cpr{\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta-\theta_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}\right)}. \end{aligned}\]

The exponent is a quadratic in $\theta$.

Reading a Gaussian off its exponent

Claim. If $p(x)\propto e^{-f(x)}$ with $f(x)=ax^2-bx+c$ and $a>0$, then $X\sim\cN\!\left(\dfrac{b}{2a},\dfrac{1}{2a}\right)$.

Proof.

\[ax^2-bx+c=\frac{\left(x-\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2}{2\cdot\frac{1}{2a}}+C,\qquad C\text{ free of }x.\]

Reading a Gaussian off its exponent

Applied to the posterior. Expanding the two squares in $\theta$,

\[a=\clk{\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}}+\cpr{\frac{1}{2\tau_0^2}},\qquad b=\clk{\frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}}+\cpr{\frac{\theta_0}{\tau_0^2}},\] \[\boxed{\cpo{\Theta\mid y_1^n}\sim\cN\!\left(\frac{\clk{\frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}}+\cpr{\frac{\theta_0}{\tau_0^2}}}{\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}+\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}},\;\frac{1}{\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}+\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}}\right)}\]
No integral needed
The coefficient of $\theta^2$ fixes the variance; the coefficient of $\theta$ fixes the mean. As $\tau_0\to\infty$ the result tends to $\cN(\bar y,\sigma^2/n)$, the flat-prior posterior.
Precisions add, and the mean is precision-weighted

Write $\Theta\mid y_1^n\sim\cN(\mu_1,\tau_1^2)$. With precision the reciprocal of variance,

\[\cpo{\frac{1}{\tau_1^2}}=\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}},\qquad\cpo{\mu_1}=\frac{\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}\,\theta_0}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}\,\bar y}}{\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}}.\]

The mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and $\bar y$, with weights the prior precision $\cpr{1/\tau_0^2}$ and the data precision $\clk{n/\sigma^2}$: the same shape as the Beta mean, whose weights were $\alpha+\beta$ and $n$.

The posterior mean is the balance point of the precisions
\[\cpo{\mu_1}=\frac{\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}\,\theta_0}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}\,\bar y}}{\cpr{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}+\clk{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}}\]

Predict: travel times with $\sigma^2=4$, prior $\theta_0=10$, $\tau_0^2=1$, and $n=4$ days with $\bar y=12$. Where does $\mu_1$ land?

Data precision $4/4=1$, prior precision $1$, posterior precision $2$: $\Theta\mid y_1^4\sim\cN(11,\,1/2)$.

The posterior is narrower than both the prior and the likelihood
Precisions add
Two sources of information about $\theta$ combine into one Gaussian whose precision is the sum of theirs and whose mean sits between them, closer to the more precise source.

The prior's precision stays fixed while the data's grows with $n$, so the prior's share of the weight goes to zero.

Two estimators of the same mean: the Bayesian one shrinks $\bar y$

Take the prior $\Theta\sim\cN(0,\tau_0^2)$. The posterior mean (also its mode and median) and the MLE:

\[\cpo{\hat\theta_B}=\bar y\left(\frac{\tau_0^2}{\tau_0^2+\sigma^2/n}\right),\qquad\cml{\hat\theta_{\mle}}=\bar y.\]

$\cpo{\hat\theta_B}$ is $\bar y$ shrunk toward the prior mean $0$ by a factor in $(0,1)$: with $\tau_0^2=1$ and $\sigma^2=4$, $1/2$ at $n=4$ and $4/5$ at $n=16$.

Frequentist view: judging both estimators over repeated samples

With the prior $\Theta\sim\cN(0,\tau_0^2)$,

\[\cpo{\hat\theta_B}=\bar y\left(\frac{\tau_0^2}{\tau_0^2+\sigma^2/n}\right),\qquad\cml{\hat\theta_{\mle}}=\bar y.\]

To compare them, judge both in the frequentist framework: a true value $\theta^*$, and each estimator a function of the random data $Y_1^n$.

Predict: which of the two is unbiased? Which has the smaller variance?

Frequentist view: bias, variance, and MSE of both

The MLE is unbiased with $\mse(\cml{\hat\theta_{\mle}})=\var(\bar Y)=\sigma^2/n$, and by the Cramér–Rao bound no unbiased estimator does better.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Bias:}\quad &\bias(\cpo{\hat\theta_B})=\E[\cpo{\hat\theta_B}]-\theta^*=-\theta^*\left(\frac{\sigma^2/n}{\tau_0^2+\sigma^2/n}\right),\\[4pt] \text{Variance:}\quad &\var(\cpo{\hat\theta_B})=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\left(\frac{\tau_0^2}{\tau_0^2+\sigma^2/n}\right)^2,\\[4pt] \text{MSE:}\quad &\mse(\cpo{\hat\theta_B})=\bias(\cpo{\hat\theta_B})^2+\var(\cpo{\hat\theta_B}). \end{aligned}\]

As $\tau_0^2$ grows, the bias term falls and the variance term rises.

A strong prior trades variance for bias
A small $\tau_0^2$ is a confident prior: the estimate is biased toward $0$ but responds less to the data, so its variance is smaller.
Frequentist view: a prior near $\theta^*$ wins; a confident one far off loses

Take $\sigma^2/n=1$. Predict: as $\tau_0^2$ runs from $0$ (the prior alone) to $\infty$ (the MLE), how do $\bias^2$ and $\var$ move? Is there a $\tau_0^2$ whose MSE falls below $\sigma^2/n=1$?

below the MLE for every $\tau_0^2>0$;
minimum $0.5$ at $\tau_0^2=1$.

above the MLE for $\tau_0^2<1.5$, below it beyond;
minimum $0.8$ at $\tau_0^2=4$.

Frequentist view: $\tau_0^2=(\theta^*)^2$ beats the MLE, but needs $\theta^*$

For $\sigma^2/n=1$, both minima sit at $\tau_0^2=(\theta^*)^2$. In general, $\tau_0^2=(\theta^*)^2$ gives

\[\begin{aligned} \mse(\cpo{\hat\theta_B})&=\frac{(\theta^*)^2\,\sigma^2/n}{(\theta^*)^2+\sigma^2/n}\\ &<\frac{\sigma^2}{n}=\mse(\cml{\hat\theta_{\mle}}). \end{aligned}\]
The right prior needs $\theta^*$, which nobody has
A biased estimator can still have the smaller MSE. A prior built from experience or related data moves the estimate the right way without knowing $\theta^*$.

Conjugate priors: the pattern behind every example

Given a likelihood, a conjugate prior is a family of distributions such that the posterior is in the same family.

LikelihoodData summaryPriorPosterior
$\ber(\theta)$$y=\sum_iy_i$$\bet(\alpha,\beta)$$\bet(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y)$
$\poi(\lambda)$$S=\sum_iy_i$$\gam(\alpha,\beta)$$\gam(\alpha+S,\beta+n)$
$\expo(\theta)$$y=\sum_iy_i$$\gam(\alpha,\beta)$$\gam(\alpha+n,\beta+y)$
$\cN(\theta,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2$ known$\bar y$$\cN(\theta_0,\tau_0^2)$$\cN(\mu_1,\tau_1^2)$

Conjugate for one observation implies conjugate for $n$ iid observations: update $n$ times, one observation at a time.

No integral
The posterior's normalizing constant is the family's own, so conjugacy determines the posterior completely.
Exponential waits: a Gamma prior gives a Gamma posterior

Waiting times between incidents, $Y_i\mid\theta\sim\expo(\theta)$, rate $\theta$ per day; $y=\sum_{i=1}^ny_i$ is the total wait.

\[\begin{aligned} \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)\propto\theta^{\htmlClass{sa}{\alpha}\htmlClass{s1}{-1}}e^{-\htmlClass{sb}{\beta}\theta}},\\ \clk{\text{Likelihood:}}\quad &\clk{p(y_1^n\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n\theta e^{-\theta y_i}=\theta^{\htmlClass{sn}{n}}e^{-\theta\htmlClass{sy}{y}}},\\ \cpo{\text{Posterior:}}\quad &\cpo{p(\theta\mid y_1^n)\propto\htmlClass{gaB}{\theta}^{\htmlClass{da}{\cpr{\alpha}}\htmlClass{gaP}{+}\htmlClass{dn}{\clk{n}}\htmlClass{d1}{\cpr{-1}}}\htmlClass{gbB}{e}^{\htmlClass{gbB}{-(}\htmlClass{db}{\cpr{\beta}}\htmlClass{gbP}{+}\htmlClass{dy}{\clk{y}}\htmlClass{gbB}{)\theta}}}\htmlClass{gbB}{.} \end{aligned}\]
\[\boxed{\cpo{\Theta\mid y_1^n\sim\gam(\cpr{\alpha}+\clk{n},\cpr{\beta}+\clk{y})}}\]
The count updates the shape; the total updates the rate
$\clk{n}$ adds to $\cpr{\alpha}$ and $\clk{y}$ adds to $\cpr{\beta}$.
Exponential waits, an example

The incident rate $\theta$ has prior $\cpr{\gam(2,2)}$, with mean $1$ per day. Then $\clk{n=5}$ waits are observed, totalling $\clk{y=10}$ days:

\[\begin{aligned} \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)\propto\theta^{2-1}e^{-2\theta}},\\ \clk{\text{Likelihood:}}\quad &\clk{p(y_1^5\mid\theta)=\theta^{5}e^{-10\theta}},\\ \cpo{\text{Posterior:}}\quad &\cpo{p(\theta\mid y_1^5)\propto\theta^{\cpr{2}+\clk{5}\cpr{-1}}e^{-(\cpr{2}+\clk{10})\theta}}. \end{aligned}\] \[\boxed{\cpo{\Theta\mid y_1^5\sim\gam(7,12)}}\]

The posterior mean $7/12=0.58$ per day lies between the prior mean $1$ and the MLE $n/y=0.5$.

The exponential family

A likelihood $p(y\mid\theta)$ is in the exponential family if

\[p(y\mid\theta)=\exp\left(\clk{a(y)}^Tb(\theta)+\clk{f(y)}+g(\theta)\right),\]

where $\clk{a},b,y,\theta$ may be vectors and $\clk{f},g$ are scalar; $b(\theta)$ is the natural parameter. Gaussian, Beta, Gamma and Binomial are members.

Your turn: write the Bernoulli pmf $\theta^y(1-\theta)^{1-y}$ in this form. What are $\clk{a}$, $b$, $\clk{f}$, $g$?

Your turn: the Bernoulli pmf in exponential-family form

Write the Bernoulli pmf $\theta^y(1-\theta)^{1-y}$ as

\[p(y\mid\theta)=\exp\left(\clk{a(y)}^Tb(\theta)+\clk{f(y)}+g(\theta)\right).\]

What are $\clk{a}$, $b$, $\clk{f}$, $g$?

Solution \[\theta^y(1-\theta)^{1-y}=\exp\big(y\ln\theta+(1-y)\ln(1-\theta)\big)=\exp\left(\clk{y}\ln\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}+\ln(1-\theta)\right),\]

so $\clk{a(y)=y}$, $b(\theta)=\ln\dfrac{\theta}{1-\theta}$ (the log odds), $\clk{f(y)=0}$, $g(\theta)=\ln(1-\theta)$.

Sufficient statistics

For $n$ iid observations,

\[\begin{aligned} p(y_1^n\mid\theta)&\propto\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\clk{a(y_i)}^Tb(\theta)+\clk{n}\,g(\theta)\right)\\ &=\exp\left(\clk{t(y_1^n)}^Tb(\theta)+\clk{n}\,g(\theta)\right), \end{aligned}\]

with $\clk{t(y_1^n)=\sum_{i=1}^na(y_i)}$, the sufficient statistic. The MLE and the posterior depend on the data only through $\clk{t}$, whose size does not grow with $n$.

All the information about $\theta$ sits in $t(y_1^n)$
The rest of the data is irrelevant for estimating $\theta$.
Sufficient statistics of the Bernoulli and the Gaussian mean

The sufficient statistic of $n$ iid observations is $\clk{t(y_1^n)=\sum_{i=1}^na(y_i)}$.

Bernoulli
$\clk{t=\sum_iy_i}$, the number of successes.
Gaussian mean, $\sigma^2$ known
$\clk{a(y)=y}$, $b(\theta)=\theta/\sigma^2$, so $\clk{t=\sum_iy_i}$ and the posterior uses only $\bar y$.
The conjugate prior of an exponential family

Match the prior to the shape of the likelihood, with two parameters $\cpr{\nu}$ and $\cpr{m}$ in the places of $\clk{t(y_1^n)}$ and $\clk{n}$:

\[\begin{aligned} \clk{\text{Likelihood:}}\quad &\clk{p(y_1^n\mid\theta)}\propto\exp\left(\clk{t(y_1^n)}^Tb(\theta)+\clk{n}\,g(\theta)\right),\\ \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)}\propto\exp\left(\cpr{\nu}^Tb(\theta)+\cpr{m}\,g(\theta)\right),\\ \cpo{\text{Posterior:}}\quad &\cpo{p(\theta\mid y_1^n)}\propto\exp\left((\cpr{\nu}+\clk{t(y_1^n)})^Tb(\theta)+(\cpr{m}+\clk{n})\,g(\theta)\right). \end{aligned}\]
One recipe for every exponential family
The posterior adds the data's statistic $\clk{t(y_1^n)}$ to $\cpr{\nu}$ and the sample size $\clk{n}$ to $\cpr{m}$.
For the Bernoulli, the conjugate prior is a Beta

Bernoulli: $b(\theta)=\ln\dfrac{\theta}{1-\theta}$, $g(\theta)=\ln(1-\theta)$, and $y=\sum_iy_i$.

\[\begin{aligned} \cpr{\text{Prior:}}\quad &\cpr{p(\theta)}\propto\exp\left(\cpr{\nu}\,b(\theta)+\cpr{m}\,g(\theta)\right)=\theta^{\cpr{\nu}}(1-\theta)^{\cpr{m}-\cpr{\nu}}\;\;\text{is }\cpr{\bet(\cpr{\nu}+1,\cpr{m}-\cpr{\nu}+1)},\\[6pt] \cpo{\text{Posterior:}}\quad &\cpo{\Theta\mid y_1^n}\sim\cpo{\bet(\cpr{\nu}+\clk{y}+1,\;\cpr{m}+\clk{n}-\cpr{\nu}-\clk{y}+1)}. \end{aligned}\]

The exponents of last time's $\bet(\alpha,\beta)$ are $\cpr{\nu}=\alpha-1$ and $\cpr{m}-\cpr{\nu}=\beta-1$: the prior looks like $\cpr{m}$ earlier observations with statistic $\cpr{\nu}$.

Summary
Gaussian mean, $\sigma^2$ known: a flat prior gives $\cN(\bar y,\sigma^2/n)$; a Gaussian prior gives a precision-weighted mean with precisions added; the predictive variance $\sigma^2+\sigma^2/n$ is noise plus estimation uncertainty.
Judged on one problem, the Bayesian estimator trades bias for variance and, with a prior near the truth, has a smaller MSE than the MLE.
The two frameworks agree with a flat prior and as $n\to\infty$.
