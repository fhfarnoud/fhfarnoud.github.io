Sampling from a Chain

Initial transient: Early samples may reflect the starting state rather than the stationary distribution. Discard an initial segment as burn-in.

Dependent samples: Consecutive states are correlated. Strong dependence reduces the information gained from each additional iteration.

Thinning: Keep every $m$-th sample to reduce dependence between retained draws. Choosing $m$ is difficult, and thinning discards information.

Burn-in reduces the influence of the starting state. Thinning reduces dependence between retained samples. Neither guarantees good mixing.

Choosing the Jump Size

For a symmetric Gaussian proposal,

\[\boldsymbol{\theta}'\sim J(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol{\theta},\tau^2I)\]

Too small

Most proposals are accepted, but the chain explores the target slowly.

Too large

Many proposals land in low-density regions and are rejected, leaving the chain unchanged.

Balance acceptance with movement across the target.

Why Small Jumps Are Slow

\[X_i=\begin{cases}+\varepsilon&\text{with probability }1/2\\-\varepsilon&\text{with probability }1/2\end{cases}\]

After $T$ independent, zero-mean steps,

\[\textstyle S_T=\sum_{i=1}^T X_i,\qquad L=\mathbb E[|S_T|]\]

The absolute value is less convenient. Compute squared displacement instead.

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[S_T^2]&=\textstyle\mathbb E\bigl[(\sum_{i=1}^T X_i)^2\bigr]\\[8pt] &=\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^T\mathbb E[X_i^2]+2\sum_{i<j}\underbrace{\mathbb E[X_iX_j]}_{=0}\\[4pt] &=T\varepsilon^2 \end{aligned}\]

Cross terms vanish by independence and zero means.

\[L\propto\sqrt{\mathbb E[S_T^2]}=\varepsilon\sqrt T,\qquad T\propto\frac{L^2}{\varepsilon^2}\]

Dashed curves show a theoretical scale, not strict bounds.

Traveling twice as far typically needs four times as many steps.
2 min. Define the independent symmetric increments, S_T, and the expected absolute distance L. The absolute value is less convenient than a second moment. Reveal the square expansion. E[X_i^2]=epsilon squared and E[X_i X_j]=E[X_i]E[X_j]=0 for distinct indices, giving T epsilon squared. RMS is the square root of that second moment. L is not exactly RMS. For large T, the central limit approximation gives L approximately sqrt(2/pi) times RMS, so both scale like epsilon sqrt(T). The proportionality statement is a large-T scaling argument, not an exact finite-T identity. Solve the scaling for T to show the quadratic distance cost. Why Gibbs Sampling?

A complicated joint distribution can be hard to sample from, even when its conditional distributions are easy to sample from.

Fix one coordinate. The joint problem becomes a draw from a one-dimensional slice.

Sample one coordinate at a time, conditioned on the others.

Gibbs Algorithm

At coordinate $j$, draw using the most recently available values of the other coordinates.

\[\theta_j^{(t)}\sim p\!\left(\theta_j\mid \underbrace{\theta_1^{(t)},\ldots,\theta_{j-1}^{(t)}}_{\text{already updated}}, \underbrace{\theta_{j+1}^{(t-1)},\ldots,\theta_d^{(t-1)}}_{\text{not yet updated}}\right)\]
Start sweep $t$ $\bigl(\theta_1^{(t-1)},\ \theta_2^{(t-1)},\ \ldots,\ \theta_d^{(t-1)}\bigr)$
Update $\theta_1$ $\bigl({\color{#2354A8}\theta_1^{(t)}},\ \theta_2^{(t-1)},\ \ldots,\ \theta_d^{(t-1)}\bigr)$
Update $\theta_2$ $\bigl({\color{#2354A8}\theta_1^{(t)}},\ {\color{#2354A8}\theta_2^{(t)}},\ \ldots,\ \theta_d^{(t-1)}\bigr)$
Continue through $\theta_d$ $\bigl({\color{#2354A8}\theta_1^{(t)}},\ {\color{#2354A8}\theta_2^{(t)}},\ \ldots,\ {\color{#2354A8}\theta_d^{(t)}}\bigr)$

Coordinate update · One conditional draw

Full sweep · One update of each of the $d$ coordinates

Record $\boldsymbol\theta^{(t)}$ after each full sweep as one joint sample.

Posterior (Gibbs target)

\[\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathbf y\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf y,\Sigma_\rho)\]

Demo: $\mathbf y=(0,0)$

\[\theta_1\mid\mathbf y\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad \theta_2\mid\mathbf y\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\]

Horizontal update · Hold $\theta_2$

\[\begin{aligned} \theta_1^{(t)}\mid\theta_2^{(t-1)}&\sim\mathcal N(\mu_1,1-\rho^2)\\ \mu_1&=y_1+\rho(\theta_2^{(t-1)}-y_2) \end{aligned}\]

Why Gibbs Works

Update coordinate $j$ of $\boldsymbol\theta$ to get $\boldsymbol\theta'$. The vector $\boldsymbol\theta_{-j}$ contains all coordinates except $j$.

\[\theta_j'\sim p(\theta_j'\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j}),\qquad \boldsymbol\theta_{-j}'=\boldsymbol\theta_{-j}.\]
Forward flow \[p(\boldsymbol\theta)\Pr(\boldsymbol\theta\to\boldsymbol\theta') =\textcolor{#762A83}{p(\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})}\, \textcolor{#2354A8}{p(\theta_j\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})}\, \textcolor{#008000}{p(\theta_j'\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})}\]
Reverse flow \[p(\boldsymbol\theta')\Pr(\boldsymbol\theta'\to\boldsymbol\theta) =\textcolor{#762A83}{p(\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})}\, \textcolor{#008000}{p(\theta_j'\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})}\, \textcolor{#2354A8}{p(\theta_j\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})}\]

Equal flows establish detailed balance for a single Gibbs coordinate update.

\[\alpha=\min\!\left(1, \frac{p(\boldsymbol\theta')\,p(\theta_j\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})} {p(\boldsymbol\theta)\,p(\theta_j'\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})} \right)=\min(1,1)=1\]

Metropolis uses acceptance and rejection to balance the flows.
Gibbs coordinate proposals already balance the flows and are always accepted.

A full Gibbs sweep preserves $p$, but need not satisfy detailed balance.
3 min. On the common slice, read the transition factors as densities with respect to the updated coordinate. The unchanged coordinates have identical point masses in both directions. Both flows vanish off that slice. Point out the three matching factors before discussing Metropolis-Hastings. In the acceptance ratio, the reverse proposal flow divided by the forward proposal flow is one wherever the forward flow is positive. A sweep preserves p because applying each update to p leaves p unchanged. The reverse of a fixed-order sweep applies the coordinate kernels in reverse order. Two reversible kernels can give different compositions depending on their order.

Gibbs Updates and Markov Blankets

Full conditional

\[\theta_j'\sim p(\theta_j\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{-j})\]

A Gibbs update conditions on all other coordinates.

Markov blanket

In a Bayesian network, a node’s Markov blanket consists of its parents, children, and the other parents of its children.

Example: Updating C

\[p(C\mid A,B,D,E)=p(C\mid A,B,D)\]

Given $A,B,D$, knowing $E$ provides no additional information about $C$.

Conditional independence can simplify Gibbs updates by reducing the variables we need to condition on.

Why HMC?

Random-walk Metropolis

Proposes jumps in random directions. Small jumps explore slowly, while large jumps are often rejected.

Gradient descent

Uses gradients to move toward a mode of the target distribution. This is optimization, not sampling.

Hamiltonian proposal

Introduce a random momentum that determines the initial direction and speed. Gradients change the momentum, bending the trajectory.

The endpoint of the trajectory is one proposal, not a sequence of samples.

The methods do different amounts of work per step.

HMC uses momentum and gradients to construct longer proposals that explore the target.

Energy and Momentum

Position $\boldsymbol\theta$ is the parameter vector we want to sample.

Potential energy $E(\boldsymbol\theta)$ is low where the target density is high.

\[p(\boldsymbol\theta)\propto e^{-E(\boldsymbol\theta)}\]

Auxiliary momentum $\boldsymbol\phi$ drives the ball. It determines kinetic energy $K$.

\[K(\boldsymbol\phi)=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\phi^\top\boldsymbol\phi,\quad H(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi)=E(\boldsymbol\theta)+K(\boldsymbol\phi)\]
\[p(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi)\propto e^{-H}=e^{-E(\boldsymbol\theta)}e^{-K(\boldsymbol\phi)}\] \[\begin{aligned} p(\boldsymbol\theta)&=\int p(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi)\,d\boldsymbol\phi\\ &\propto e^{-E(\boldsymbol\theta)}\underbrace{\int e^{-K(\boldsymbol\phi)}\,d\boldsymbol\phi}_{\text{constant in }\boldsymbol\theta} \end{aligned}\]
Momentum allows us to construct trajectories without changing the target distribution.

3 min. Show a ball in the one-dimensional Gaussian potential E(theta)=theta squared over 2. The mode is at the valley floor. Momentum is an auxiliary variable with the same number of entries as theta. With unit mass the momentum is also the rate of change of the position coordinate. The arrow shows the signed velocity along that coordinate. Each slider sets an initial condition and returns the ball to time zero. Play uses the exact harmonic motion for this potential. The joint density factors into independent position and momentum densities. Integrating over momentum multiplies the position factor by a constant and leaves the normalized marginal p(theta).

Hamiltonian Dynamics

Together, position and momentum $(\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi)$ specify a point in phase space.

\[\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}=\boldsymbol\phi,\qquad \dot{\boldsymbol\phi}=-\nabla E(\boldsymbol\theta)\]

The dots denote derivatives with respect to simulation time.

  • Blue momentum arrow shows the current direction and speed of motion, $\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}=\boldsymbol\phi$.
  • Red force arrow shows the force $\dot{\boldsymbol\phi}=-\nabla E(\boldsymbol\theta)$, which changes the momentum and points toward lower potential energy.
  • Green curve traces the position.
\[\frac{dH}{dt}=\nabla E^\top\boldsymbol\phi+\boldsymbol\phi^\top(-\nabla E)=0\]
Momentum drives motion, gradients bend the trajectory, and total energy remains constant.

The energy bar shows how potential and kinetic energy trade off.

Leapfrog Updates

In practice, we cannot simulate Hamiltonian dynamics perfectly, so we approximate the trajectory using discrete steps.

\[\boldsymbol\phi\leftarrow\boldsymbol\phi-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla E(\boldsymbol\theta)\]

1. Half momentum update with the gradient at the current position.

\[\boldsymbol\theta\leftarrow\boldsymbol\theta+\varepsilon\boldsymbol\phi\]

2. Full position update using the updated momentum.

\[\boldsymbol\phi\leftarrow\boldsymbol\phi-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla E(\boldsymbol\theta)\]

3. Half momentum update with the gradient at the new position.

$\varepsilon$ is the integration step size and $L$ counts full leapfrog steps.

Leapfrog introduces numerical error, so total energy is not conserved exactly. A Metropolis accept/reject step corrects the resulting sampling bias.

Compare $\Delta H=H-H_0$ at completed steps. Exact dynamics give $\Delta H=0$.

HMC Algorithm

  1. Refresh momentum
    $\boldsymbol\phi_0\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I)$
  2. Generate a proposal
    Take $L$ leapfrog steps to obtain $(\boldsymbol\theta^*,\boldsymbol\phi^*)$.
  3. Accept or reject
    Correct for numerical energy error. \[\alpha=\min\left(1,e^{H_{\mathrm{start}}-H_{\mathrm{end}}}\right)\] $H_{\mathrm{start}}$ uses the refreshed momentum.
    $H_{\mathrm{end}}=H(\boldsymbol\theta^*,\boldsymbol\phi^*)$.
  4. Record the resulting position
    Keep the old position after a rejection.
HMC combines momentum-driven proposals with a Metropolis correction to sample from the target distribution.
