Parameter Estimation: Complexity

The curse of dimensionality: A general joint distribution over $m$ variables, each with $k$ values, requires: $k^m - 1$ parameters!

Example: $m = 100$ binary variables: $2^{100} \approx 10^{30}$ parameters!

Graphical models reduce this dramatically:

  • BN: $\sum_{i=1}^m k^{|\text{pa}(x_i)|}(k-1) \approx m \cdot k^{d+1}$, $d = \max_i |\text{pa}(x_i)|$
    Example: $m=100$, binary, $d=3$: only $100 \times 2^4 = 1600$ parameters
  • MRF: $\sum_{C} k^{|C|} \approx (\text{\# cliques}) \times k^{c}$, $c$ = max clique size

Learning complexity:

  • BNs: Each CPD can be learned separately $\to$ simple counting!
  • MRFs: Parameters coupled through $Z(\theta)$ $\to$ complex but can still use sampling or other computational methods.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Goal: Find parameters $\btheta$ that maximize the probability of the data $\cD$.

\[\hat{\btheta}_{\text{MLE}} = \argmax_{\btheta} p(\cD; \btheta)\]

Likelihood Function: Assuming i.i.d. samples $\cD = \{\bx^{(1)}, \dots, \bx^{(N)}\}$:

\[L(\btheta) = \prod_{i=1}^N p(\bx^{(i)}; \btheta)\]

Log-Likelihood: Easier to maximize sum than product:

\[\ell(\btheta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(\bx^{(i)}; \btheta)\]

For Bayesian Networks: $p(\bx;\btheta) = \prod_j p(x_j | \pa(x_j); \btheta_j)$

\[\ell(\btheta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^m \log p(x_j^{(i)} | \pa(x_j)^{(i)}; \btheta_j)\]

MLE Decomposition

Rearranging the sums:

\[\ell(\btheta) = \sum_{j=1}^m \underbrace{\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N \log p(x_j^{(i)} | \pa(x_j)^{(i)}; \btheta_j) \right]}_{\substack{\text{Local Log-Likelihood} \\ \text{for node } j}}\]

Key Insight:

  • The global objective decomposes into a sum of local objectives.
  • We can maximize each term independently!

Implication: To learn parameters for node $X_j$, we only need data for:

  1. $X_j$ itself and
  2. its parents $\pa(X_j)$

MLE Solution: Just Counting!

Model: Discrete variables. Parameter $\theta_{jlk} = p(X_j = k | \pa(X_j) = l)$.

Constraint: $\sum_k \theta_{jlk} = 1$ for each parent config $l$.

Solution:

\[\hat{\theta}_{jlk} = \frac{N_{jlk}}{\sum_{k'} N_{jlk'}} = \frac{N_{jlk}}{N_{jl}}\]

where:

  • $N_{jlk}$: Count of times $X_j=k$ AND $\pa(X_j)=l$ in dataset.
  • $N_{jl}$: Count of times $\pa(X_j)=l$.

Example: Traffic ($T$) $\to$ Alice Late ($A$)

  • Data: 100 days. 70 days $T=0$. Alice late ($A=1$) on 10 of those.
  • $\hat{\theta}_{A=1|T=0} = \frac{10}{70} = 0.14$.

Bayesian Parameter Estimation

Bayesian View: Parameters are random variables! We add them to the graph as parents of the variables they govern.

Prior: $p(\btheta) = p(\theta_T)p(\btheta_A)p(\btheta_B)$ (no incoming edges to parameter nodes)

Plate Notation

With $N$ samples, the graph has one copy of $T, A, B$ per sample, all sharing the parameters. For $N = 3$:

\[p(\cD, \btheta) = p(\btheta) \prod_{i=1}^{3} p(T_i|\theta_T)\, p(A_i|T_i, \btheta_A)\, p(B_i|T_i, \btheta_B)\]

Plate: a box around one copy stands for all the copies, as the product over $i$ does; the number in its corner counts them, and an edge from outside the box goes to every copy.

Posterior Distribution

Given data $\cD$ ($N$ samples), we want $p(\btheta | \cD)$.

Decomposition: By Markov blanket,

\[p(\btheta|\cD) = p(\theta_T|T_1^N) p(\btheta_A|A_1^N, T_1^N) p(\btheta_B|B_1^N, T_1^N)\]

Implication: To learn distribution of $\btheta_j$ corresponding to node $X_j$, we need data for:

  1. $X_j$ itself and
  2. its parents $\pa(X_j)$

Predicting Future Outcomes

We can also predict the next observation $(T_{n+1}, A_{n+1}, B_{n+1})$.

Prediction: Two options

  1. True Bayes: Integrate out parameters $p(A_{n+1}|T_{n+1}, \cD) = \int p(A_{n+1}|T_{n+1}, \btheta_A) p(\btheta_A|\cD) d\btheta_A$
  2. Use point estimate (e.g., MAP)

Inference: What and Why?

Setup: We now have a trained model with all parameters (In the Bayesian case, parameters become part of the graph).

Inference question: What is $p(\qry{\text{query}} | \evd{\text{evidence}})$?

Example: Traffic network with Rain 🌧️

  • Evidence: Bob is late $\evd{B=1}$ 🏃‍♂️
  • Query: Is traffic heavy? $p(\qry{T=1} | \evd{B=1})$ 🚥

The Challenge: Exponential Complexity

⚠️ Problem: Naive computation is exponential!

To compute $p(\qry{T=1} | \evd{B=1})$:

\[p(\qry{T=1} | \evd{B=1}) = \frac{p(\qry{T=1},\evd{B=1})}{p(\evd{B=1})}=\frac{\sum_{r,c} p(R=r, \qry{T=1}, C=c, \evd{B=1})}{\sum_{r,t,c} p(R=r, T=t, C=c, \evd{B=1})}\]

Must sum over all combinations of $R$ and $C$:

General case: $m$ hidden binary variables $\to$ $2^m$ combinations

💡 Solution: Exploit graph structure to avoid redundant computation

Simplifying the Problem

Goal: Compute posterior $p(\qry{T} | \evd{B=1})$.

\[p(\qry{T=t} | \evd{B=1}) = \frac{p(\qry{T=t}, \evd{B=1})}{p(\evd{B=1})}\]

Observation: The denominator $p(\evd{B=1})$ is the same for all $t$.

\[p(\evd{B=1}) = \sum_{t'} p(T=t', \evd{B=1})\]

Strategy:

  1. Compute the numerator (joint probability) for each value of $\qry{T}$: \[\alpha_0 = p(\qry{T=0}, \evd{B=1}), \quad \alpha_1 = p(\qry{T=1}, \evd{B=1})\]
  2. Normalize to get probabilities: \[p(\qry{T=1} | \evd{B=1}) = \frac{\alpha_1}{\alpha_0 + \alpha_1}\]

Conclusion: Only compute joint probability $p(\qry{T}, \evd{B=1})$.
Same approach works for unconditional queries, e.g., $p(\qry{T})$.

Variable Elimination: A Concrete Example

Task: Compute both numerators $\alpha_t = p(\qry{T=t}, \evd{B=1})$, $t \in \{0, 1\}$, efficiently.

Naive Summation: $\alpha_t = \sum_c \sum_r p(R=r, \qry{T=t}, C=c, \evd{B=1})$

Step 1: Factorize according to the graph:

\[\alpha_t = \sum_c \sum_r p(R=r)\, p(\qry{T=t}|R=r)\, p(C=c)\, p(\evd{B=1}|\qry{T=t}, C=c)\]

Step 2: Eliminate $R$ (Sum over $r$): Move the sum over $r$ inside, next to the terms involving $R$:

\[\alpha_t = \sum_c p(C=c) p(\evd{B=1}|\qry{T=t}, C=c) \underbrace{\left[ \sum_r p(R=r) p(\qry{T=t}|R=r) \right]}_{\msg{m(\qry{T=t})}}\]

Step 3: Eliminate $C$ (Sum over $c$): Use the result $\msg{m(\qry{T=t})}$ in the remaining sum:

\[\alpha_t = \msg{m(\qry{T=t})} \sum_c p(C=c) p(\evd{B=1}|\qry{T=t}, C=c)\]

Result: For each $t$, replaced a sum over $R \times C$ pairs with two smaller sums.

Elimination order matters

Example: Chain $x_1 \to x_2 \to x_3 \to \qry{x_4}$. Compute $p(\qry{x_4})$.

\[\begin{aligned} p(\qry{x_4}) &= \sum_{x_1,x_2,x_3}p(x_1)p(x_2|x_1)p(x_3|x_2)p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) \\ &\htmlClass{u2}{{}= \sum_{x_3}\sum_{x_2}\sum_{x_1}p(x_1)p(x_2|x_1)p(x_3|x_2)p(\qry{x_4}|x_3)} \\ &\htmlClass{u3}{{}= \sum_{x_3} p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) \sum_{x_2} p(x_3|x_2) \underbrace{\sum_{x_1} p(x_1) p(x_2|x_1)}_{\msg{m_1(x_2)}}} \\ &\htmlClass{u4}{{}= \sum_{x_3} p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) \underbrace{\sum_{x_2} p(x_3|x_2) \msg{m_1(x_2)}}_{\msg{m_2(x_3)}}} \\ &\htmlClass{u5}{{}= \sum_{x_3} p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) \msg{m_2(x_3)}} \end{aligned}\]

Now only $O(k^2)$ operations! Massive savings compared to naive $k^4$.

Bad Elimination Order

Same Example: Chain $x_1 \to x_2 \to x_3 \to \qry{x_4}$. Compute $p(\qry{x_4})$.

Bad Idea: Eliminate $x_2$ first

\[\begin{aligned} p(\qry{x_4}) &= \sum_{x_1,x_2,x_3}p(x_1)p(x_2|x_1)p(x_3|x_2)p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) \\ &= \sum_{x_1}\sum_{x_3}p(x_1)p(\qry{x_4}|x_3)\sum_{x_2}p(x_2|x_1)p(x_3|x_2) \\ &= \sum_{x_1} p(x_1) \sum_{x_3} p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) \msg{\underbrace{\sum_{x_2} p(x_2|x_1) p(x_3|x_2)}_{m_1(x_1, x_3)}} \\ &= \sum_{x_1} p(x_1) \msg{\underbrace{\sum_{x_3} p(\qry{x_4}|x_3) m_1(x_1, x_3)}_{m_2(x_1, \qry{x_4})}} \\ &= \sum_{x_1} p(x_1) \msg{m_2(x_1, \qry{x_4})} \end{aligned}\]

Problem: Computing $\msg{m_1}$ requires summing over $x_2$ for each pair $(x_1, x_3)$! Complexity $O(k^3)$.

Variable Elimination: The General Algorithm
(For Reference)

The Recipe: To compute $p(\qry{X_{\text{query}}} | \evd{X_{\text{evidence}}})$:

  1. Initialize: Start with all factors (or CPDs) in the graph.
  2. Evidence: Fix values of observed variables (sum contains a single term).
  3. Order: Choose an elimination ordering for hidden variables.
  4. Eliminate: For each variable $V$ in the ordering:
    • Collect: Find all factors that mention $V$. Let $q$ be the set of variables other than $V$ in these factors.
    • Multiply: Multiply them to form a temporary factor $\psi(V,q)$.
    • Sum: Find $\msg{m(q)}=\sum_v \psi(V=v,q)$ for all possible values of $q$.
    • Update: Replace old factors with $\msg{m(q)}$.
  5. Normalize: Multiply remaining factors and normalize.

Note: Complexity depends on elimination order! Finding optimal order is NP-hard.

Moralization: From a BN to an MRF

Goal: message passing, coming up, is stated for MRFs. To run it on a BN, first write the BN as an MRF.

Each CPD becomes a potential on $x_i$ and its parents, so they must form a clique: $p(B|T,C)$ needs the clique $\{T, C, B\}$.

Moralization:

  1. Marry the parents: join every two parents of a common child, here $T$ and $C$.
  2. Drop the directions.

Potentials: $\psi(R) = p(R)$, $\psi(R,T) = p(T|R)$, $\psi(C) = p(C)$, $\psi(T,C,B) = p(B|T,C)$; their product is $p$, so $Z = 1$.

Lost independence: in the BN, $T \independent C$: the only path between them, $T \to B \leftarrow C$, meets the unobserved collider $B$. The distribution is unchanged, but in the moral graph $T$ and $C$ are adjacent, so the graph no longer shows $T \independent C$.

Factor Graphs

One MRF graph, two factorizations: in both, every pair of $T, C, B$ shares a potential, so both give the moral graph’s triangle.

(a) one potential: $\psi(T,C,B)$

(b) three potentials: $\psi(T,C)\,\psi(T,B)\,\psi(C,B)$

Factor graph: variable nodes (circles) and factor nodes (squares); each factor $f$ is joined to the variables it depends on, $x_f$, and $p(\bx) \propto \prod_{f} f(x_f)$.

MRF to factor graph: one factor node per potential, joined to its variables.

The two factor graphs differ: (a) has no cycle; (b) has the cycle $T$, $\psi(T,C)$, $C$, $\psi(C,B)$, $B$, $\psi(T,B)$. The traffic network is (a), with $\psi(T,C,B) = p(B|T,C)$.

From a BN to a Factor Graph

BN to factor graph: one factor node per CPD $p(x_i | \pa(x_i))$, joined to $x_i$ and its parents.

Example: the traffic network, $p(R,T,C,B) = p(R)\,p(T|R)\,p(C)\,p(B|T,C)$: four factors.

The directions are gone, as in the moral graph, but $p(B|T,C)$ stays one node, joined to $T$, $C$ and $B$; $p(R)$ and $p(C)$ are joined to one variable each.

The CPDs multiply to a normalized distribution, so $\propto$ becomes $=$: $p(\bx) = \prod_f f(x_f)$, with $Z = 1$.

Moral Graph vs. Factor Graph: Which Is a Tree?

Message passing, coming up, is exact on a tree: a connected graph with no cycle. A connected graph on $n$ nodes is a tree exactly when it has $n-1$ edges.

Moral graph: 4 nodes, 4 edges

Factor graph: 4 + 4 nodes, 7 edges

Moral graph: not a tree. Marrying $T$ and $C$ closed the cycle $T, B, C$; a node with two parents always does, since it and its parents form a triangle.

Factor graph: a tree; $T$, $C$ and $B$ are joined only through the one factor node $p(B|T,C)$.

Variable Elimination as Messages on the Factor Graph

Query $\qry{T}$, evidence $\evd{B=1}$, on the factor graph of the traffic network.

Branches: removing $T$ splits the tree in two, one part through $p(T|R)$ and one through $p(B|T,C)$.

The two sums of variable elimination are one per branch; $\msg{m_1(\qry{t})}$ is the $\msg{m(\qry{T=t})}$ of “Variable Elimination: A Concrete Example”:

\[\alpha_t = p(\qry{T=t}, \evd{B=1}) = \underbrace{\sum_r p(R=r)\,p(\qry{T=t}|R=r)}_{\msg{m_1(\qry{t})}}\;\htmlClass{u2}{\underbrace{\sum_c p(C=c)\,p(\evd{B=1}|\qry{T=t},C=c)}_{\msg{m_2(\qry{t})}}}\]

Each sum uses only its branch’s factors and reaches $\qry{T}$ along one edge: a message. On any tree, eliminating from the leaves toward the query is passing such messages.

Sum-Product Algorithm: Message Passing on Trees

💡 Key idea: On trees, we can find optimal elimination order by systematically passing messages towards the query node.

Example: Tree MRF, compute marginal at $\qry{x_4}$.

Messages flow toward query node $\qry{x_4}$:

  • $\msg{m_{1 \to 3}(x_3)} = \sum_{x_1} \psi(x_1, x_3)$
  • $\msg{m_{2 \to 3}(x_3)} = \sum_{x_2} \psi(x_2, x_3)$
  • $\msg{m_{3 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})} = \sum_{x_3} \psi(x_3, \qry{x_4}) \cdot \msg{m_{1 \to 3}(x_3)} \cdot \msg{m_{2 \to 3}(x_3)}$
  • $\msg{m_{5 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})} = \sum_{x_5} \psi(\qry{x_4}, x_5)$

Final marginal: $p(\qry{x_4}) \propto \msg{m_{3 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})} \cdot \msg{m_{5 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})}$

Reading the Sum-Product Messages

Intuition (roughly speaking):

  • $\msg{m_{2 \to 3}(x_3)}$: how plausible each value of $x_3$ is from $x_2$'s point of view, with every value of $x_2$ considered.
  • $\msg{m_{3 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})}$: how plausible each value of $\qry{x_4}$ is from the point of view of $x_3$'s subtree $\{x_1, x_2, x_3\}$, with every configuration of the subtree considered: \[\msg{m_{3 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})} = \sum_{x_1, x_2, x_3} \psi(x_1, x_3)\, \psi(x_2, x_3)\, \psi(x_3, \qry{x_4})\]

Messages are weights, not probabilities: only their ratios across values matter.

Sum-Product Algorithm
(For Reference)

Message from node $i$ to node $j$:

\[\msg{m_{i \to j}(x_j)} = \sum_{x_i} \psi(x_i) \psi(x_i, x_j) \prod_{k \in N(i) \setminus j} \msg{m_{k \to i}(x_i)}\]

where $N(i)$ = neighbors of node $i$

Marginal at node $j$ (after all messages received):

\[p(\qry{x_j}) \propto \psi(\qry{x_j}) \prod_{i \in N(j)} \msg{m_{i \to j}(\qry{x_j})}\]

All marginals on tree MRFs: Send all possible messages on each edge in both directions.

For general graphs: keep passing messages in loops: loopy belief propagation (approximate)

Finding the Most Likely Configuration

Different question: Instead of probabilities, find best configuration

Example: Image denoising

  • Observed: Noisy image pixels
  • Goal: Find most likely clean image
\[\qry{\bx^*} = \argmax_{\qry{\bx}} p(\qry{\bx} | \evd{\text{noisy pixels}})\]

💡 Key insight: Replace $\sum$ with $\max$!

Sum-product:

\[\msg{m_{i \to j}} = \sum_{x_i} \cdots\]

Computes probabilities

Max-product:

\[\msg{m_{i \to j}} = \max_{x_i} \cdots\]

Finds best config

Bonus: Need to backtrack to recover actual configuration

Reading the Max-Product Messages

Intuition (roughly speaking):

Sum-product: $\msg{m_{i \to j}(x_j)}$: how plausible each value of $x_j$ is from $i$'s side, all configurations of that side added up

Max-product: $\msg{m_{i \to j}(x_j)}$: how plausible each value of $x_j$ is from $i$'s side, counting only that side's best configuration

Example: on the tree above, $\msg{m_{3 \to 4}(\qry{x_4})} = \max_{x_1, x_2, x_3} \psi(x_1, x_3)\, \psi(x_2, x_3)\, \psi(x_3, \qry{x_4})$: the weight of the best configuration of $x_3$'s subtree for each value of $\qry{x_4}$.

Max-Product Algorithm
(For Reference)

Message from node $i$ to node $j$:

\[\msg{m_{i \to j}(x_j)} = \max_{x_i} \psi(x_i) \psi(x_i, x_j) \prod_{k \in N(i) \setminus j} \msg{m_{k \to i}(x_i)}\]

Find maximum at node $j$:

\[\max_{\qry{x_j}} p(\qry{x_j}) = \max_{\qry{x_j}} \left[\psi(\qry{x_j}) \prod_{i \in N(j)} \msg{m_{i \to j}(\qry{x_j})}\right]\]

Backtracking to recover $\qry{\bx^*}$:

  1. Store: $\psi_j(x_j) = \argmax_{x_i} [\msg{\text{message terms}}]$ during forward pass
  2. Start at any node: $\qry{x_j^*} = \argmax_{\qry{x_j}} p(\qry{x_j})$
  3. Trace back using stored pointers $\psi_i$

Application: Viterbi algorithm for HMMs (special case of max-product on chain)

Inference in Hidden Markov Models

Model: hidden states $x_1^T = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$ form a Markov chain; each observation $\evd{y_t}$ depends on $x_t$ alone:

\[p(x_1^T, \evd{y_1^T}) = p(x_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} p(x_t | x_{t-1}) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(\evd{y_t} | x_t)\]

Evidence: with $\evd{y_1^T}$ observed, each $p(\evd{y_t}|x_t)$ is a function of $x_t$ alone, a node potential $\psi(x_t)$; $\psi(x_1)$ also takes the factor $p(x_1)$:

\[p(x_1^T | \evd{y_1^T}) \propto \prod_{t=1}^{T} \psi(x_t) \prod_{t=2}^{T} \psi(x_{t-1}, x_t), \qquad \psi(x_{t-1}, x_t) = p(x_t | x_{t-1})\]

This is an MRF on the chain of hidden states (right), a tree, so the sum-product and max-product algorithms apply:

  • Posterior marginal $p(\qry{x_t} | \evd{y_1^T})$: sum-product, the forward-backward algorithm
  • Most likely sequence $\argmax_{\qry{x_1^T}} p(\qry{x_1^T} | \evd{y_1^T})$: max-product, the Viterbi algorithm

Sum-Product on an HMM: The Forward-Backward Algorithm

Query: $p(\qry{x_t} | \evd{y_1^T})$. On the chain each node has at most two neighbors, so the product in the sum-product message has at most one factor:

Forward (from $x_1$; none into $x_1$):

\[\msg{m_{t-1\to t}(x_t)} = \sum_{x_{t-1}} \psi(x_{t-1})\, p(x_t | x_{t-1})\, \msg{m_{t-2\to t-1}(x_{t-1})}\]

Backward (from $x_T$; none into $x_T$):

\[\msg{m_{t+1\to t}(x_t)} = \sum_{x_{t+1}} \psi(x_{t+1})\, p(x_{t+1} | x_t)\, \msg{m_{t+2\to t+1}(x_{t+1})}\]

Marginal, its three factors read as probabilities:

\[p(\qry{x_t} | \evd{y_1^T}) \propto \underbrace{\msg{m_{t-1\to t}(\qry{x_t})}}_{p(\qry{x_t},\, \evd{y_1^{t-1}})}\, \underbrace{\psi(\qry{x_t})}_{p(\evd{y_t} | \qry{x_t})}\, \underbrace{\msg{m_{t+1\to t}(\qry{x_t})}}_{p(\evd{y_{t+1}^T} | \qry{x_t})} = p(\qry{x_t}, \evd{y_1^T})\]

Normalizing divides by $p(\evd{y_1^T})$. One pass each way gives the messages at every $t$, and so every marginal: $O(Tk^2)$ with $k$ values for each $x_t$, against $k^T$ sequences in the naive sum.

Forward messages, by induction. With $\psi(x_1) = p(x_1)\,p(\evd{y_1}|x_1)$,

\[\msg{m_{1\to2}(x_2)} = \sum_{x_1} p(x_1)\, p(\evd{y_1}|x_1)\, p(x_2|x_1) = p(x_2, \evd{y_1}).\]

If $\msg{m_{t-2\to t-1}(x_{t-1})} = p(x_{t-1}, \evd{y_1^{t-2}})$, then, since $\evd{y_{t-1}}$ depends only on $x_{t-1}$ and $x_t$ only on $x_{t-1}$,

\[\begin{aligned}\msg{m_{t-1\to t}(x_t)} &= \sum_{x_{t-1}} p(x_{t-1}, \evd{y_1^{t-2}})\, p(\evd{y_{t-1}}|x_{t-1})\, p(x_t|x_{t-1}) \\ &= \sum_{x_{t-1}} p(x_{t-1}, x_t, \evd{y_1^{t-1}}) = p(x_t, \evd{y_1^{t-1}}).\end{aligned}\]

Backward messages, by induction from $t = T-1$ down: $\msg{m_{T\to T-1}(x_{T-1})} = \sum_{x_T} p(\evd{y_T}|x_T)\, p(x_T|x_{T-1}) = p(\evd{y_T}|x_{T-1})$, and if $\msg{m_{t+2\to t+1}(x_{t+1})} = p(\evd{y_{t+2}^T}|x_{t+1})$,

\[\msg{m_{t+1\to t}(x_t)} = \sum_{x_{t+1}} p(\evd{y_{t+1}}|x_{t+1})\, p(x_{t+1}|x_t)\, p(\evd{y_{t+2}^T}|x_{t+1}) = p(\evd{y_{t+1}^T}|x_t).\]

The product: given $x_t$, the evidence before $t$, at $t$ and after $t$ are independent ($x_t$ separates them on the chain), so

\[p(x_t, \evd{y_1^{t-1}})\, p(\evd{y_t}|x_t)\, p(\evd{y_{t+1}^T}|x_t) = p(x_t, \evd{y_1^T}).\]

Notation: Ch. 12 writes $\alpha_t(i) = \msg{m_{t-1\to t}(x_t{=}i)}$ and $\beta_t(i) = \msg{m_{t+1\to t}(x_t{=}i)}$; many texts take $\alpha_t(i) = p(x_t{=}i, \evd{y_1^t})$, which includes $\psi(x_t)$.

Forward-Backward: A Worked Example

Example: on days $t = 1, 2, 3$ a patient is healthy or sick, $x_t \in \{H, S\}$, with $p(x_1{=}H) = 0.5$, and coughs or not, $\evd{y_t} \in \{\evd{C}, \evd{N}\}$. Observed: $\evd{y_1^3 = (N, C, N)}$, a cough on day 2 only.

Trellis: in each node $\psi(x_t) = p(\evd{y_t}|x_t)$ (on day 1 times $0.5$); left of it the forward message into it, right of it the backward one.

Forward: $\msg{m_{1\to2}(H)} = 0.4 \cdot 0.75 + 0.2 \cdot 0.25 = \msg{0.35}$

$\msg{m_{2\to3}(H)} = 0.2 \cdot 0.75 \cdot \msg{0.35} + 0.6 \cdot 0.25 \cdot \msg{0.25} = \msg{0.09}$

Backward: $\msg{m_{3\to2}(H)} = 0.8 \cdot 0.75 + 0.4 \cdot 0.25 = \msg{0.7}$

$\msg{m_{2\to1}(H)} = 0.2 \cdot 0.75 \cdot \msg{0.7} + 0.6 \cdot 0.25 \cdot \msg{0.5} = \msg{0.18}$

Marginals (between the rows): on day 2, $\msg{0.35} \cdot 0.2 \cdot \msg{0.7} = 0.049$ and $\msg{0.25} \cdot 0.6 \cdot \msg{0.5} = 0.075$, so $p(\qry{x_2{=}S} | \evd{y_1^3}) = 0.075 / 0.124 = \qry{0.60}$. Each day's two products sum to $p(\evd{y_1^3}) = 0.124$.

Max-Product on an HMM: The Viterbi Algorithm

Query: the most likely sequence $(\qry{x_1^*}, \dots, \qry{x_T^*}) = \argmax_{x_1^T} p(x_1^T | \evd{y_1^T})$, which also maximizes $p(x_1^T, \evd{y_1^T}) = p(x_1^T | \evd{y_1^T})\, p(\evd{y_1^T})$.

Forward (toward $x_T$; none into $x_1$), the forward message with the sum replaced by a max:

\[\msg{m_{t-1\to t}(x_t)} = \max_{x_{t-1}} \psi(x_{t-1})\, p(x_t | x_{t-1})\, \msg{m_{t-2\to t-1}(x_{t-1})} = \max_{x_1^{t-1}} p(x_1^{t-1}, x_t, \evd{y_1^{t-1}})\]

Back-pointer: $b_t(x_t)$, the $x_{t-1}$ that attains this max, kept for each value of $x_t$.

Backtrack: $\qry{x_T^*} = \argmax_{x_T} \psi(x_T)\, \msg{m_{T-1\to T}(x_T)}$, then $\qry{x_{t-1}^*} = b_t(\qry{x_t^*})$ for $t = T, \dots, 2$.

Cost: one pass, $O(Tk^2)$ with $k$ values for each $x_t$, against comparing $k^T$ sequences.

The max-product messages, by induction, as the forward messages with $\max$ in place of $\sum$:

\[\msg{m_{1\to2}(x_2)} = \max_{x_1} p(x_1)\, p(\evd{y_1}|x_1)\, p(x_2|x_1) = \max_{x_1} p(x_1, x_2, \evd{y_1}).\]

If $\msg{m_{t-2\to t-1}(x_{t-1})} = \max_{x_1^{t-2}} p(x_1^{t-1}, \evd{y_1^{t-2}})$, then, since the factors $p(\evd{y_{t-1}}|x_{t-1})\, p(x_t|x_{t-1})$ do not depend on $x_1^{t-2}$ and are not negative,

\[\begin{aligned}\msg{m_{t-1\to t}(x_t)} &= \max_{x_{t-1}} p(\evd{y_{t-1}}|x_{t-1})\, p(x_t|x_{t-1}) \max_{x_1^{t-2}} p(x_1^{t-1}, \evd{y_1^{t-2}}) \\ &= \max_{x_1^{t-1}} p(x_1^{t-1}, x_t, \evd{y_1^{t-1}}).\end{aligned}\]

Backtracking: $\psi(x_T)\, \msg{m_{T-1\to T}(x_T)} = \max_{x_1^{T-1}} p(x_1^T, \evd{y_1^T})$, the probability of the best sequence that ends in $x_T$ (with the evidence); its max over $x_T$ is that of the best sequence, and $\qry{x_T^*}$ is its last state. The best sequence that ends in $x_t$ has $b_t(x_t)$ as its state at $t-1$, and its part up to $t-1$ is the best sequence that ends there; so $b_t(\qry{x_t^*})$ is the best sequence's state at $t - 1$.

Notation: Ch. 12 writes the max-product message as $\delta_t(i) = \msg{m_{t-1\to t}(x_t{=}i)}$; the slide “Max-Product Algorithm” calls the stored pointers $\psi_j$.

Viterbi: A Worked Example

Same example: $p(x_1{=}H) = 0.5$ and $\evd{y_1^3 = (N, C, N)}$. Which sequence of states is most likely?

Trellis: in each node $\psi(x_t)$; left of it the max-product message into it; a thick edge is a back-pointer.

$\msg{m_{1\to2}(H)} = \max(0.4 \cdot 0.75,\, 0.2 \cdot 0.25)$
$= \max(0.3,\, 0.05) = \msg{0.3}$, so $b_2(H) = H$

$\msg{m_{2\to3}(H)} = \max(0.2 \cdot 0.75 \cdot \msg{0.3},\, 0.6 \cdot 0.25 \cdot \msg{0.15})$
$= \max(0.045,\, 0.0225) = \msg{0.045}$, so $b_3(H) = H$

$\max_{x_3} \psi(x_3)\, \msg{m_{2\to3}(x_3)} = \max(0.8 \cdot \msg{0.045},\, 0.4 \cdot \msg{0.0675})$
$= \max(0.036,\, 0.027)$, so $\qry{x_3^* = H}$

Backtrack: $\qry{x_2^*} = b_3(H) = H$, $\qry{x_1^*} = b_2(H) = H$; the sequence $\qry{H, H, H}$ has $p(\qry{HHH}, \evd{y_1^3}) = 0.036$

The most likely state of each day (ringed; forward-backward) gives $H, S, H$, with $p(HSH, \evd{y_1^3}) = 0.012$: here the most likely sequence is not the sequence of most likely states.
