Byte pair encoding

A language model reads text as tokens, pieces from a fixed vocabulary that byte pair encoding builds by counting.

Learned once, from a training corpus: start from characters (bytes, in current models); repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token; keep the merges in order.

Merge 1: e + s, 9 times (newest 6, widest 3).

Merge 2: es + t, 9 times.

Merge 3: l + o, 7 times (low 5, lower 2).

Merge 4: lo + w, 7 times.

low is one token and est a shared ending; the rarer words stay in pieces. A language model does not read words or letters directly. It reads tokens, pieces of text from a fixed vocabulary, and byte pair encoding builds that vocabulary by counting. The tokenizer is learned once, from a training corpus, before the language model itself is trained. It starts from single characters, single bytes in current models, and repeatedly merges the pair of neighbouring symbols that occurs most often in the corpus. Here the training corpus is a toy of four words, each with its count. Count every adjacent pair, weighting each word by how often it occurs. The pair e s occurs in both newest and widest, which makes it the most frequent pair, tied with s t. Merging it makes es a token of its own, everywhere it appears. Counting again, es followed by t is now the most frequent pair, so est becomes a token: the shared ending of newest and widest. Next come l o and o w, from low and lower, again tied. Merging l and o first gives lo. Merging lo with w makes low a single token. The frequent word is now one symbol, while lower, newest and widest still take several tokens each. A real tokenizer runs tens of thousands of merges on a large corpus, so common words end up as single tokens and rare words as a few pieces. The list of merges, in the order they were learned, is what the tokenizer keeps.

Tokenizing new text

The tokenizer cuts any new text by applying its merges in the order they were learned.

Take lowest, a word the corpus of slide 5 does not contain. Start from its characters and apply the four merges in order.

It ends as two tokens of the vocabulary, low and est.

newer contains none of the merged pairs, so it stays as five characters.

The vocabulary comes from the training corpus; a new text is never counted, only cut. The tokenizer cuts every new text with its list of merges, applied in the order they were learned. Take lowest, a word that never appears in the corpus of the previous slide. It starts as its six characters. The first merge joins e and s, which occur next to each other in lowest too. The second joins es and t into the ending est. The third joins l and o. The fourth joins lo and w. Lowest ends as two tokens of the vocabulary, low and est, although the corpus never contained this word. A word with none of the merged pairs, like newer, gets no merges and stays as single characters. Real vocabularies are large enough that most text is cut into common pieces. So the vocabulary is learned once, from the training corpus, and a new text is only cut with it, never counted.

Tokenization in practice

Real tokenizers learn tens of thousands of merges from a large sample of text, and their pieces decide what a model sees.

Meta's open Llama 3 language models use a vocabulary of 128K tokens; SmolLM2's (right) has 49,152.

Where a word is cut depends on how often its pieces appeared in the tokenizer's training text, not on its parts: strawberry is one token, Strawberry two, and three at the start of a sentence, where no space precedes it; unbelievably is cut into pieces that are not its parts. Digits are single tokens.

 Tokenization decides what the model actually sees. Vocabularies in current models are large, over a hundred thousand tokens in Llama 3, built by merges like these from a large sample of text in many languages. Another common tokenizer, the unigram tokenizer, is instead a probability model over pieces, fit by the EM algorithm of Chapter 7. On the right, a sentence of common words, cut by SmolLM2's tokenizer: each word is one token. Where a word is cut depends on how often its pieces appeared in the tokenizer's training text, not on the word's parts. In this tokenizer, strawberry in lower case is a single token. With a capital letter it becomes two, and at the start of a sentence, where no space comes before it, three. So the same word can reach the model as different tokens, and the model has to learn that they mean the same thing. An unusual word is cut into pieces that are not its parts, and the digits of a number are separate tokens. The model receives tokens, not letters.

Numbers and languages in tokens

The cuts of slide 7 follow the tokenizer's counts, and they change how a model handles numbers and what a text costs in each language.

Numbers

OpenAI's chat models GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 cut a long number into chunks of three digits from the left (Singh and Strouse 2024): 1234567 as 1234567.

Written with commas, the same number is cut from the right, 1,234,567, and their arithmetic is much better.

Languages

Languages pay unequally: the same text can take up to 15 times as many tokens in one language as in another (Petrov et al. 2023).

The cuts a tokenizer makes have consequences beyond the vocabulary. How numbers are cut into tokens affects arithmetic. OpenAI's chat models GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 have a token for every number of up to three digits, and they cut a longer number into chunks of three starting from the left, so the last chunk can be short and the chunks do not line up with thousands. Writing the number with commas makes the chunks run from the right instead, matching place value. Singh and Strouse found that the same arithmetic problems, written this way, were solved far more often. Tokenizers trained mostly on English cut other languages into many more pieces. Petrov and coauthors measured the same text, translated, taking many times as many tokens in one language as in another, so it costs more to process and fills the context sooner.

Perplexity per word

A model's perplexity per token depends on how its tokenizer cuts the text.

For a text of $n$ tokens and $W$ words with total loss in bits

\[L=\sum_{t=1}^n\log_2\frac{1}{p(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})},\]

the perplexity per token is $2^{L/n}$, and per word $2^{L/W}$.

Tokens done: the map pulls back and enters embeddings. That finishes tokens. Byte pair encoding builds the vocabulary by counting pairs, so frequent strings become single tokens. Next we give each token a vector, its embedding, learned from the contexts it appears in, so that tokens with similar meanings get nearby vectors.

Words as symbols in an n-gram model

A token is an index, and the n-gram models of Sec. 1.3 use it as nothing more.

An n-gram model estimates $p(w_t\mid w_{t-2},w_{t-1})$ (here $n=3$) by counting. To it, “cat” and “kitten” are unrelated symbols: counts after “the cat” say nothing about what follows “the kitten”.

With $V$ the vocabulary size, the one-hot vector $\be_w\in\{0,1\}^V$ of word $w$ has a single 1, at $w$'s position. Any two different words are orthogonal and equally far apart.

What a symbol model learns about one word says nothing about any other word. The language models of Section 1.3 predict a token from the previous few by counting how often each continuation followed that context. To such a model, every word is a symbol. The training text may contain many sentences about a cat and few about a kitten; the counts for the cat then say nothing about what follows kitten. One way to see this: write each word as a one-hot vector, as long as the vocabulary, with a single one in the word's own position. Any two different words then have inner product zero and the same distance, so cat is as far from kitten as it is from truck. So what the model learns about one word cannot transfer to another. We want vectors in which similar words are close.

The distributional hypothesis

Vectors in which similar words are close need a notion of when two words are similar, and linguistics supplies one.

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps!”

The distributional hypothesis: words that occur in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.

Goal: a vector $\bu_w\in\bbR^D$ for every word $w$, $D$ from 50 to 300, with similar words close together. In real word vectors learned from Wikipedia and news text (right), closeness (cosine similarity, next slide) is graded: from “cat”, dog 0.88, kitten 0.56, car 0.31, truck 0.23.

Two questions: how to measure closeness, and how to learn the vectors from text.

 To give words vectors, we need a working notion of when two words are similar. The linguist J. R. Firth put it as a slogan in 1957: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Its modern form is called the distributional hypothesis: words that occur in similar contexts, such as cat and kitten, tend to have similar meanings. So we look for a vector for every word, with a few dozen to a few hundred coordinates, such that words used in similar contexts get nearby vectors. On the right are real vectors of this kind, from a model we will meet shortly: the bars compare the vector of cat with four others, using a measure of closeness we define on the next slide. Closeness is graded: the two animals score well above the two vehicles, and dog scores higher than kitten. This raises two questions. How do we measure that two vectors are close? And how do we learn such vectors from text? We take them in this order.

Word2vec: the CBOW model

Word2vec turns the distributional hypothesis into a task: predict a hidden word (right) from its context.

Each word $w$ of the vocabulary $\cV$ has a context vector $\bc_w$ and an output vector $\bu_w$.

CBOW (continuous bag of words), with context the $m/2$ words on each side of the blank in its sentence (here $m=4$):

  1. average their vectors: $\bh=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\bc_{w_j}$;
  2. score every $w\in\cV$ by $\langle\bu_w,\bh\rangle$;
  3. softmax over $\cV$: $\;p(w\mid\text{context})=\dfrac{e^{\langle\bu_w,\bh\rangle}}{\sum_{w'\in\cV}e^{\langle\bu_{w'},\bh\rangle}}$.
The features are $\bh$; word $w$ is a class with weights $\bu_w$ (Ch. 6). Word2vec learns embeddings from a fill-in-the-blank task. Hide a word in a sentence and predict it from the words around it, its context: the words within a fixed window of positions on each side, in the same sentence. Vectors that predict hidden words well must place words used in similar contexts near each other, which is the property we want. Each word gets two vectors: a context vector, for when it appears around the blank, and an output vector, for when it is the word being predicted. The continuous bag-of-words model, or CBOW, makes the prediction in three steps, as in the diagram. Here the window is two words on each side of the blank, so the context is store, bought, from and grocery. CBOW averages the context vectors of the words around the blank into one vector, h. It scores every word of the vocabulary by the inner product of that word's output vector with h. Softmax over the vocabulary turns the scores into probabilities. This is softmax regression from Chapter 6: h plays the role of the features, and each vocabulary word is a class with its own weight vector, its output vector. The difference is that the features are learned too: the context vectors are parameters, fit together with the output vectors.

Gradients of the CBOW loss

Training fits every vector by maximum likelihood, one blank at a time, with gradients from the chain rule.

For a blank with hidden word $w^*$, scores $z_w=\langle\bu_w,\bh\rangle$ and their softmax $p(w\mid\text{context})$ over $\cV$:

\[\cL=-\ln p(w^*\mid\text{context})=-z_{w^*}+\ln\sum_{w'\in\cV}e^{z_{w'}}.\]

Its derivative with respect to a score is the error (Ch. 6), with $I(w=w^*)=1$ only for the hidden word:

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial\cL}{\partial z_w}&=-I(w=w^*)+\frac{e^{z_w}}{\sum_{w'}e^{z_{w'}}}\\&=\htmlClass{hl-err}{p(w\mid\text{context})-I(w=w^*)}.\end{aligned}\]

Since $\partial z_w/\partial\bu_w=\bh$, the gradient is error times input:

\[\nabla_{\bu_w}\cL=\big(p(w\mid\text{context})-I(w=w^*)\big)\,\bh.\]

$\bh=(0.375,\,0.325,\,0.225)$; $p=0.541$ for oranges, $0.459$ for laptops; $\cL=-\ln0.541=0.614$.

Errors: $0.541-1=-0.459$ for oranges, $0.459$ for laptops.

Output-vector gradients:

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla_{\bu_{\text{oranges}}}\cL&=-0.459\,\bh\\&=(-0.172,\,-0.149,\,-0.103),\\\nabla_{\bu_{\text{laptops}}}\cL&=0.459\,\bh\\&=(0.172,\,0.149,\,0.103).\end{aligned}\]
Training fits the vectors by maximum likelihood, one blank at a time. The loss of a blank is the negative log of the probability the model gives the hidden word. Written out, it is minus the hidden word's score plus the log of the softmax denominator, a sum over the whole vocabulary. On the right is the store sentence's blank from the last slide: the hidden word is oranges, and the model gives it a little over half of its probability. Now differentiate with respect to one score. The first term gives minus one, for the hidden word only; the second gives that word's softmax probability. Their sum, the predicted probability minus the indicator of the hidden word, is the error, as in softmax regression. In the example, oranges has a negative error, since the model gave it less than certainty, and laptops a positive error of the same size. Each score depends on its own output vector only through an inner product with h, so the gradient for an output vector is its error times h: error times input. In the example the two gradients point in opposite directions along h, so a step against them moves the output vector of oranges toward h and that of laptops away from it.

Learning the context vectors

The context vectors are learned by the same gradient descent, through their average $\bh=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\bc_{w_j}$.

$\bh$ enters every score $z_w=\langle\bu_w,\bh\rangle$:

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla_{\bh}\cL&=\sum_{w\in\cV}\big(p(w\mid\text{context})-I(w=w^*)\big)\,\bu_w\\&=\sum_{w\in\cV}p(w\mid\text{context})\,\bu_w-\bu_{w^*}.\end{aligned}\]

Each context word's vector gets $1/m$ of it:

\[\nabla_{\bc_{w_j}}\cL=\tfrac1m\nabla_{\bh}\cL=\tfrac1m\big(\textstyle\sum_wp(w\mid\text{context})\,\bu_w-\bu_{w^*}\big).\]

A step moves each $\bc_{w_j}$ toward $\bu_{w^*}$ and away from the probability-weighted average of the output vectors.

A word's $\bc_w$ is updated in every blank where the word is in the context, so over the corpus it moves toward the output vectors of the words it tends to surround.

Errors $-0.459$ (oranges) and $0.459$ (laptops); $m=4$.

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla_{\bh}\cL&=-0.459\,\bu_{\text{oranges}}+0.459\,\bu_{\text{laptops}}\\&=0.459\,(\bu_{\text{laptops}}-\bu_{\text{oranges}})\\&=(-0.275,\,-0.138,\,0.321).\end{aligned}\]

Each of the four context vectors (store, bought, from, grocery):

\[\begin{aligned}\nabla_{\bc_{w_j}}\cL&=\tfrac14\nabla_{\bh}\cL\\&=(-0.069,\,-0.034,\,0.080).\end{aligned}\]
The context vectors are learned by the same gradient descent as the output vectors. They reach the loss only through h, the average of the context words' vectors, so we first find the gradient for h and then share it out among them. Unlike an output vector, h enters every score, so its gradient sums the error of each word times that word's output vector. Since the errors are the probabilities minus the indicator, this is the probability-weighted average of the output vectors minus the hidden word's output vector. With two candidate words, it is a multiple of the difference between the output vectors of laptops and oranges. Each context word's vector contributes one m-th of h, so it gets one m-th of h's gradient; in the example, each of the four context vectors gets a quarter. A step against this gradient moves every context vector toward the hidden word's output vector and away from the probability-weighted average of all the output vectors. This is how a context vector is learned. A word's context vector changes in every blank where the word appears around the hidden word, each time a little toward that hidden word's output vector. Over the corpus, it takes in the words it tends to surround, the company it keeps.

Training CBOW by gradient descent

Training starts from random context vectors (in gensim, a widely used open-source word2vec library, output vectors at zero) and takes one step of size $\eta$ per blank:

\[\bu_w\leftarrow\bu_w-\eta\,\nabla_{\bu_w}\cL,\qquad\bc_{w_j}\leftarrow\bc_{w_j}-\tfrac{\eta}{m}\,\nabla_{\bh}\cL,\]

the same step for every context word $j=1,\dots,m$ of the blank.

One step on slide 16's blank, $\eta=0.5$: $\bu_{\text{oranges}}$ becomes $(0.786,0.575,0.252)$ and $\bu_{\text{laptops}}$ $(0.014,0.125,0.848)$, every context vector moves by the same $(0.034,0.017,-0.040)$, and $p(\text{oranges}\mid\text{context})$ rises from 0.541 to 0.589.

Skip-gram with negative sampling

Negative sampling replaces CBOW's sum over the whole vocabulary by a few random words.

Skip-gram predicts each context word $c$ of a word $w$ (a word within the window around $w$ in its sentence) from $w$. With negative sampling (SGNS), this is logistic regression on (word, context) pairs: label 1 for a pair from the text, 0 for each of $k$ pairs with a random context:

\[p(\text{label}=1\mid w,c)=\sigma(\langle\bu_w,\bc_c\rangle),\]

with $\sigma$ the sigmoid (Ch. 6) and $\bc_c$ the context vector of $c$.

The loss of a real pair with random contexts $c_1,\dots,c_k$:

\[-\ln\sigma(\langle\bu_w,\bc_c\rangle)-\sum_{i=1}^k\ln\sigma(-\langle\bu_w,\bc_{c_i}\rangle).\]
The softmax of CBOW needs a sum over the whole vocabulary for every blank, which is expensive. Negative sampling avoids it, and its best-known form is skip-gram with negative sampling. Take a word and one of the words within its window, a context word, and call the pair real. Draw k random context words for the same word and call those pairs fake, as on the right. A logistic regression on the inner product of the two vectors learns to tell real pairs from fake ones. The loss of one real pair is the negative log probability of calling it real, plus, for each random context, the negative log probability of calling that pair fake. Only k plus one inner products are computed, however large the vocabulary.

Pointwise mutual information and the SGNS optimum

Levy and Goldberg (NIPS 2014) worked out what the fitted SGNS vectors converge to.

With enough coordinates to fit every pair, the SGNS loss is minimized by setting $\langle\bu_w,\bc_c\rangle=\PMI(w,c)-\ln k$ for every pair $(w,c)$, where

\[\htmlClass{hl-pmi}{\PMI(w,c)=\ln\frac{p(w,c)}{p(w)\,p(c)}},\]

$p(w,c)$ is the fraction of all (word, context) pairs, counted within the window over the whole corpus, that are $(w,c)$, and $p(w)$, $p(c)$ are its marginals.

Example: $p(w,c)=0.02$, $p(w)=0.1$, $p(c)=0.05$ give $\PMI(w,c)=\ln4$.

$\langle\bu_w,\bc_c\rangle$ is fit to $\PMI(w,c)-\ln k$; averaged over pairs, $\PMI$ is the mutual information $I(W;C)$ of Sec. 1.4.

A word $w$ occurs with context $c$ with probability $p(w,c)$, and each occurrence of $w$ draws $k$ random contexts from $p(c)$. Per pair of the text, the expected objective for $(w,c)$, with $x=\langle\bu_w,\bc_c\rangle$, is

\[\ell(x)=p(w,c)\ln\sigma(x)+k\,p(w)\,p(c)\ln\sigma(-x).\]

With $\frac{d}{dx}\ln\sigma(x)=\sigma(-x)$, setting $\ell'(x)=0$ gives

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{p(w,c)\,\sigma(-x)=k\,p(w)\,p(c)\,\sigma(x).}\]

Since $\sigma(x)/\sigma(-x)=e^x$,

\[\htmlClass{dk2}{x=\ln\frac{p(w,c)}{k\,p(w)\,p(c)}=\PMI(w,c)-\ln k.}\]

This needs each inner product to be free to take its own value, which holds when the vectors have enough coordinates (Levy and Goldberg, Sec. 3.1). With fewer, the fit is a weighted low-rank approximation of this matrix.

 What does this fit converge to? Levy and Goldberg showed that, when the vectors have enough coordinates to fit every pair exactly, the best inner product for each pair is its pointwise mutual information, shifted down by the log of k. Pointwise mutual information compares how often the word and the context occur together with how often they would if they were independent. Together means within the window: every pair of a word and a context word in its window, counted over the whole corpus. In the example, the joint probability is larger than the product of the two marginals, so the pair occurs more often than chance would predict and its pointwise mutual information is positive. So the embeddings are a compressed table of pointwise mutual information. Averaged over pairs, pointwise mutual information is the mutual information between a word and its context, from Section 1.4. Words with similar rows in this table, that is, similar company, get similar vectors. The theorem assumes a perfect fit; the next slide checks it on a real text.

Analogies by vector arithmetic

Differences between vectors carry meaning too: the same offset recurs across many pairs of words.

To answer “$a$ is to $b$ as $c$ is to ?”, take the word nearest by cosine to $\bu_b-\bu_a+\bu_c$ (unit vectors), leaving out $a$, $b$ and $c$. Right: king − man + woman.

Attention as a weighted average

Attention gives each word a new vector, a weighted average of the vectors of all the words in its sentence.

\[\bv_i^{\text{new}}=\sum_{j=1}^n\htmlClass{hl-a}{\cat{\alpha_{ij}}}\,\bv_j,\qquad\cat{\alpha_{ij}}\ge0,\quad\sum_j\cat{\alpha_{ij}}=1.\]

Here $\cat{\balpha_i}=(\cat{\alpha_{i1}},\dots,\cat{\alpha_{in}})$ is a distribution over the $n$ positions, and $\bv_i^{\text{new}}$ is the mean of $\bv_J$ for $J\sim\cat{\balpha_i}$.

Example: “old spring couch soft” (right), with given weights.

\[\begin{aligned}\bv^{\text{new}}_{\text{spring}}&=0.1(1,0)+0.1(1,1)+0.5(1,3)+0.3(0,2)\\&=(0.70,\,2.20).\end{aligned}\]

Spring's own vector is not lost to its small weight: a transformer block adds this output to it, $\bx+f(\bx)$ (slide 43). How much a token attends to itself varies by head.

Attention builds a new vector for each word as a weighted average of the vectors of all the words in the sentence. The weights are nonnegative and add to one, so for each word they form a probability distribution over the positions of the sentence, and the new vector is the expected value of the vectors under that distribution. Take the phrase old spring couch soft, with the two-dimensional vectors on the right. Suppose spring puts most of its weight on couch and soft. For now these weights are given; we will compute them shortly. The area of each circle is proportional to its weight. The new vector for spring is the weighted average, marked by the star. It has moved from spring's own vector toward couch and soft, the furniture sense of the word. Spring's own vector is not lost, although its weight is small: the transformer block adds the attention output to the word's vector, a residual connection we will meet later. In trained models, how much a token attends to itself differs from head to head.

Query, key and value

So far the weights were given; a transformer computes them from the vectors themselves.

Learned $\mW_Q,\mW_K\in\bbR^{d\times D}$ and $\mW_V\in\bbR^{d_v\times D}$ turn each input $\bx_j\in\bbR^D$ into a query, a key and a value:

\[\underset{\textsf{what $i$ looks for}}{\cq{\bq_i}=\mW_Q\bx_i}\qquad\underset{\textsf{what $j$ is matched on}}{\ck{\bk_j}=\mW_K\bx_j}\qquad\underset{\textsf{what $j$ contributes}}{\cv{\bv_j}=\mW_V\bx_j}\] \[\htmlClass{hl-s}{s_{ij}=\frac{\langle\cq{\bq_i},\ck{\bk_j}\rangle}{\sqrt d}},\qquad\cat{\balpha_i}=\softmax_{j=1,\dots,n}(s_{ij}).\]

Example: slide 32's vectors, $\mW_Q=\mW_K=\mW_V=\mI$, $D=d=2$. For “spring”, $\langle\cq{\bq_{\text{spring}}},\ck{\bk_j}\rangle=(2,5,7,2)$ for $j=1,\dots,4$, and $\cat{\balpha_{\text{spring}}}=(0.022,0.187,0.768,0.022)$ (top right),

so $\bv^{\text{new}}_{\text{spring}}=(1.93,\,2.54)$, against slide 33's $(1.30,2.20)$ (bottom right).

 Where do the weights come from? Each position starts from an input vector. Three learned matrices turn it into a query, which says what the position is looking for, a key, which says what it offers to be matched on, and a value, which is what it contributes to the average when it is matched. These roles are a reading of a soft lookup: the query is matched against every key, and the values of the matches are averaged. Training only fits the three matrices to lower the loss, so a given head need not use them this way. The score of position j for position i is the inner product of i's query with j's key, divided by the square root of the key length, and softmax over the positions turns each row of scores into weights. To see the computation, take the season sentence with all three matrices equal to the identity, so queries, keys and values are the word vectors themselves. The scores for spring, at the top of the figure, are its inner products with the four vectors, largest for season. Softmax turns them into the weights below them, produced by the model rather than given. The weighted average of the values is the new vector, the solid star at the bottom. It lies closer to season than the dashed star from the given weights, because the softmax put more weight on season. In a trained model the three matrices are learned by gradient descent, so the model learns what to attend to.

Variance of an attention score

Slide 34 divides each score by $\sqrt d$; the reason is the size of an inner product of long vectors.

Suppose the $d$ components of a query $\cq{\bq_i}$ and a key $\ck{\bk_j}$ are independent, with mean 0 and variance 1 (Vaswani et al. 2017).

Each product $q_{i\ell}k_{j\ell}$ then has mean 0 and variance $\E[q_{i\ell}^2k_{j\ell}^2]=\E[q_{i\ell}^2]\,\E[k_{j\ell}^2]=1$, and the variances of independent terms add (Ch. 0):

\[\Var(\langle\cq{\bq_i},\ck{\bk_j}\rangle)=\sum_{\ell=1}^d\Var(q_{i\ell}k_{j\ell})=d.\]

So the scores spread like $\sqrt d$: a standard deviation of 8 at $d=64$ (right, one random draw).

Question: What does the softmax do with scores this far apart?

 Why does slide 34 divide the scores by the square root of d? The reason is the size of an inner product between long vectors. Suppose the coordinates of a query and a key are independent, with mean zero and variance one: the assumption Vaswani and coauthors make when they introduce the factor. The product of a query coordinate with the matching key coordinate then has mean zero, and since the two are independent, its variance is the product of their variances, one. The score adds d such products, and the variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances, so the score has variance d. Its standard deviation, the square root of d, is the typical size of a score. On the right are the scores of one random query against sixteen random keys: most lie within one standard deviation of zero, and a few go well beyond it. Long vectors therefore give large scores, far apart from one another. Predict what the softmax does with scores like these before going on.

Scaled dot-product attention

Scores this far apart saturate the softmax, and a saturated softmax passes back almost no gradient.

The softmax grows exponentially in its scores, so one weight comes near 1 and the rest near 0 (right: slide 35's draw, unscaled; over 2,000 draws the largest weight averages 0.84).

From $\cat{\alpha_{ij}}=e^{s_{ij}}/\sum_\ell e^{s_{i\ell}}$, by the quotient rule,

\[\frac{\partial\cat{\alpha_{ij}}}{\partial s_{ij}}=\cat{\alpha_{ij}}-\cat{\alpha_{ij}}^2=\cat{\alpha_{ij}}(1-\cat{\alpha_{ij}}),\]

near 0 when $\cat{\alpha_{ij}}$ is near 0 or 1: little gradient reaches the scores, or through them $\mW_Q$ and $\mW_K$.

Dividing by $\sqrt d$ gives the scores variance 1: the largest weight averages 0.25, the weights stay spread, and the gradients stay useful.

 divide by $\sqrt{64}$ The softmax exponentiates the scores, so a score a few units above the others takes nearly all the weight. On the right is the draw of the last slide, unscaled: one key gets almost all the weight, and over many draws the largest weight is close to one on average. Even in the two-dimensional example of slide 34, dropping the factor makes the weights sharper. This matters for training. Differentiating a weight with respect to its own score gives the weight times one minus the weight. When the weight is near zero or near one, this is near zero, so a saturated softmax passes almost no gradient back to the scores, and through them to the query and key matrices. Dividing by the square root of d gives the scores variance one again. The weights stay spread out, and the softmax stays where its gradients are useful. The causal mask

A language model predicts each token from the ones before it, so attention must not look ahead.

With the scores $s_{ij}=\langle\cq{\bq_i},\ck{\bk_j}\rangle/\sqrt d$ of slide 34, set

\[\begin{aligned}&s_{ij}=-\infty\ \text{for}\ j>i\ \text{(before the softmax)},\\&\text{so}\ e^{s_{ij}}=0\ \text{and}\ \cat{\alpha_{ij}}=0.\end{aligned}\]

Right: slide 34's weights, without the mask.

With the mask, they become lower triangular.

One pass over a text gives every $p(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})$, so the loss $\cL=-\sum_t\ln p(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})$ comes from all positions at once.

Every factor of $p(w_{1:n})=\prod_tp(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})$ (Sec. 1.3) comes from one pass. A language model predicts each token from the ones before it, so during training a position must not see the words that follow it. The causal mask enforces this. The scores are the query-key products of slide thirty-four, divided by the square root of d; before the softmax, every score for a later position is set to minus infinity, so its exponential, and its weight, are zero. On the right are the attention weights of the season example, one row per position, still without the mask. With the mask, everything above the diagonal is zero, and each row spreads its weight over the current and earlier positions only. The first word can attend only to itself. Because each row sees only its past, the output at each position can predict the next token from exactly the tokens so far. One pass over a training text gives the predicted probability of every token from its prefix, and the loss, the sum of the negative log probabilities, is computed for all positions at once. This is the chain rule of Section 1.3 built into the network: every factor of the probability of the text, each token given its prefix, comes out of the same forward pass.

Positional encoding

Attention so far ignores where each word sits, so word order must be supplied separately.

So position $t$ is encoded, by

  • a learned vector $\bp_t$ added to $\bx_t$;
  • sinusoids of $t$ (Vaswani et al. 2017);
  • queries and keys rotated by angles proportional to $t$ (rotary embeddings, used by Meta's Llama 2 models and SmolLM2).
 Attention by itself ignores word order. Without the mask, each output is a weighted average whose weights depend only on the vectors, not on where the words sit, so shuffling the words shuffles the outputs and changes nothing else. Dog bites man and man bites dog would look the same. On the right is the season example: the weights that spring puts on each word, and its new vector. In the season example with the words reversed, spring puts the same weights on the same words and gets the same new vector. So order has to be added. The usual ways add a position vector to each input, learned or built from sinusoids of the position, or rotate each query and key by an angle that grows with the position, so that the score between two words depends on how far apart they are.

The transformer block

A transformer stacks $L$ blocks of the same form, one per layer, and every block contains masked multi-head attention.

A block has two parts:

  1. masked multi-head attention;
  2. an MLP (the neural-networks lecture) at each position;

each with a residual connection, $\bx+f(\bx)$ for the part $f$, and layer normalization (each vector rescaled to mean 0 and variance 1 across its coordinates). The last layer is softmax regression over the vocabulary.

Parameters per block, of width $d$: attention $4d^2$, MLP $8d^2$, so about $12d^2$.

With $d=4096$ and $L=32$, $32\times12d^2\approx6.4$B; with the embeddings, about $6.7$B: the shape of Llama 2 7B.

Attention: $\mW_Q,\mW_K,\mW_V$ (all heads together) and the output matrix $\mW_O$ are $d\times d$ each: $4d^2$.

MLP: $d\to4d\to d$, two matrices of $4d^2$ each: $8d^2$. Biases and normalization add only multiples of $d$.

At $d=4096$: $12d^2=201{,}326{,}592$ per block, $6{,}442{,}450{,}944$ for 32 blocks.

Llama 2 7B's configuration (hidden size 4,096; 32 layers; MLP width 11,008 with three matrices; vocabulary 32,000): attention $4d^2=67.1$M and MLP $3\times4096\times11{,}008=135.3$M per block, $202.4$M in all; $6.48$B for 32 blocks; input and output embeddings $2\times32{,}000\times4{,}096=0.26$B; total $6.74$B.

Llama 2's paper: “apply pre-normalization using RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019), use the SwiGLU activation function (Shazeer, 2020)”. RMSNorm rescales each vector without subtracting its mean; SwiGLU is why its MLP has three matrices.

 A transformer is a stack of blocks of the same form, one per layer. Each block has two parts. Masked multi-head attention lets each position gather information from the positions before it. Then a small neural network, the MLP, processes each position on its own. Each part adds its output to its input, a residual connection, so information can pass through the block unchanged, and a layer normalization keeps the vectors at a stable scale. After the last block, a linear layer and a softmax give the next-token probabilities: the softmax regression of Chapter 6. Where are the parameters? Attention needs four square matrices of the model's width, for queries, keys, values and the output that joins the heads. The MLP widens each vector fourfold and narrows it back, which takes twice as many. That is where the count on the slide comes from. With the width and depth of Llama 2's smallest model, the blocks give the count on the slide, and the token embeddings at the input and the output add the rest. This is the size behind the name of a seven-billion-parameter model.

The KV cache

Generating text runs the transformer once per new token, and the causal mask lets most of that work be reused.

With the mask, earlier positions never see later ones, so their keys and values never change.

They are stored, in the KV cache: each new token computes its own query, key and value, attends to the cache, and joins it.

Memory per token: $2\times\text{layers}\times\text{width}$ numbers. In 16-bit numbers, with 32 layers of width 4,096: 0.5 MB per token, and 2 GB for a context of 4,096 tokens.

 Generation runs the model once per new token. Because of the causal mask, the keys and values of earlier tokens do not change when a token is appended, so recomputing them would be wasted work. The model stores them, in what is called the KV cache. Each new token computes its own query, key and value, attends to everything in the cache, and adds its key and value to it, as the next step on the right shows. Once rain is processed, its key and value join the cache, and the next token, today, attends to all four. The cache grows with every token, and the price is memory. Every token stores one key and one value in every layer, each as long as the model's width. For a model the size of Llama 2's smallest, that gives the amount on the slide per token, and a few thousand tokens of context fill gigabytes. Memory grows in proportion to the length of the context.

Attention over long contexts

Each new token attends to the whole context, so a relevant token competes with every other one.

In this toy, one key matches the query well and all the others match poorly, by a fixed margin. As the context grows, the many weak matches together take over: the relevant key's weight, plotted on the right, falls from nearly all of the attention to a small share. To hold the relevant key at half the weight, its score must beat the others by the log of the number of competitors, so the scores have to grow with the context length. Autoregressive generation and temperature

Generation runs the chain rule of Sec. 1.3 forward, one sampled token at a time.

\[p(w_{1:n})=\prod_{t=1}^np(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})\]

Generate: sample $w_t$ from $p(\cdot\mid w_{1:t-1})$, append it, repeat.

Temperature $T>0$ divides the logits $z_k$ (Ch. 6): $\;\cpd{p_T(k)}=\dfrac{e^{z_k/T}}{\sum_je^{z_j/T}}$.

Generation is the chain rule run forward. The probability of a text is the product of each token's probability given the tokens before it, and the transformer computes each of those factors. To generate, the model computes the distribution of the next token, samples one, appends it to the context, and repeats, as the loop on the right shows. Each step reuses the KV cache. Temperature, from Chapter 6, reshapes each next-token distribution by dividing the logits by T before the softmax. At T equal to one we sample from the model itself; the bars use the toy rain, snow and clear logits from that chapter. Below one, temperature sharpens the distribution toward the most probable token; as T goes to zero, sampling becomes picking the most probable token every time. Above one, it flattens the distribution toward uniform, so less likely tokens appear more often. Other decoding rules, and why the most probable text is not typical text, come in the lecture on decoding.

Summary

Byte pair encoding builds the vocabulary by counting: frequent strings become tokens, rare words stay in pieces; models with different tokenizers are compared by perplexity per word.
CBOW and skip-gram are softmax and logistic regression with learned features; SGNS inner products estimate $\PMI-\ln k$, on a real text too.
Attention: a softmax of query and key scores weights the values; the causal mask computes every conditional in one pass.
A transformer stacks blocks of about $12d^2$ parameters; generation samples one token at a time from the chain rule.

Tokens come from counting, embeddings from PMI, attention from a softmax, and generation from the chain rule.
