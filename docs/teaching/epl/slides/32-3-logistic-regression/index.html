Classification starts with an input, a vector of features, and a label from a finite set of classes. We observe training examples whose labels are known. The goal is to use those examples to learn a rule that predicts the label of a new input. For rain tomorrow, the features can be today's humidity and pressure, and the label is zero for a dry day or one for a rainy day. For the next word, the input represents the text so far and the label is one token from a vocabulary of K tokens. The same setup covers both examples.

A probabilistic approach

A purely predictive classifier outputs only a label $\htmlClass{hl-yhat}{\hat y(\bx)}$. A probabilistic classifier estimates $\htmlClass{hl-pyx}{p(y\mid\bx)}$ for each label, then predicts the most probable one.

  • Probabilities say how sure the prediction is.
  • Smooth dependence on the parameters lets gradient descent fit the model.
  • Sampling generates data, such as the text an LLM writes.

Suppose we know $p(y\mid\bx)$. Predict the most probable class:

\[\hat y(\bx)=\htmlClass{hl-argmax}{\arg\max_j\,p(y=j\mid\bx)}.\]

This minimizes the probability of misclassification, \[\mathcal L=\htmlClass{hl-risk}{P\big(\hat y(\bX)\neq Y\big)}.\]

Given $\bX=\bx$, the prediction $\hat y(\bx)$ is wrong with probability $1-p\big(y=\hat y(\bx)\mid\bx\big)$.

This is smallest when $p\big(y=\hat y(\bx)\mid\bx\big)$ is largest, that is, for the most probable class.

Averaging over $\bX$,

\[P\big(\hat y(\bX)\neq Y\big)=\E\Big[1-p\big(y=\hat y(\bX)\mid\bX\big)\Big],\]

which is smallest when every term is.

 A purely predictive classifier gives us just a label for each input. A probabilistic classifier gives the probability of each possible label instead. For rain, we need the probability of rain; for the next token, one probability for each token in the vocabulary. We can still predict the most probable label. These probabilities say how sure the prediction is, so a decision can weigh them against its costs. They also give us a way to learn and a way to generate. Smooth dependence on the parameters lets gradient descent fit the model, while the number of mistakes changes in jumps. Sampling from the probabilities generates data: a language model writes text by sampling each next token. Suppose we already know the probability of each class for a given input. (We will learn how to estimate these probabilities later.) The curve on the right shows the probability of rain from afternoon humidity. We predict rain where that probability exceeds one half and no rain otherwise (here this happens at humidity equal to 70%). In general, we pick the class with the highest probability. This rule minimizes the chance of misclassification.

Two modeling approaches

Two ways to obtain $p(y\mid\bx)$:

Generative: model how the data arise
1. Prior$p(y)$
2. Class-conditionals$p(\bx\mid y)$
3. Posterior$p(y\mid\bx)$

Bayes' rule: $\htmlClass{hl-bayes}{p(y\mid\bx)=p(y)p(\bx\mid y)/p(\bx)}$.

Examples: LDA, QDA, naive Bayes.

Discriminative: model the posterior directly

Parametrize $p(y\mid\bx)$ without modeling $p(\bx\mid y)$.

Example: logistic regression.

There are two ways to arrive at the probability of a label given the input. The generative way models how common each class is and how the inputs of each class are spread, and then turns these around with Bayes' rule. Because it describes the inputs themselves, such a model can also generate new data. The discriminative way skips the distribution of the inputs and models the probability of the label directly, as one curve.

Which label is more likely at 70% humidity?

Example (illustrative): $y=1$ for rain tomorrow; $x$ is the afternoon humidity in percent.

Priors: $\pi=p(y=1)=\frac13$ and $p(y=0)=\frac23$.

Class-conditional densities:

\[\begin{aligned}X\mid Y=1&\sim\htmlClass{hl-rain}{\cN(80,10^2)},\\ X\mid Y=0&\sim\cN(60,10^2).\end{aligned}\]

Joint densities: $\htmlClass{hl-joint}{w_j(x)=p(y=j)\,p(x\mid y=j)=p(x,y=j)}$. By Bayes' rule $\htmlClass{hl-bayes}{p(y=j\mid x)=w_j(x)/p(x)}$, so the class with the larger $w_j(x)$ is the more likely one.

At $x=70$:

\[\begin{aligned}w_1(70)&=\tfrac13\,p(70\mid y=1)=0.0081,\\ w_0(70)&=\tfrac23\,p(70\mid y=0)=0.0161.\end{aligned}\]

Since $\htmlClass{hl-cmp}{w_0(70)>w_1(70)}$, predict no rain.

We now follow the generative route on one input, the afternoon humidity, to predict rain tomorrow. Its first ingredient is the prior, how common each label is before the humidity is observed. Here dry days are twice as common as rainy ones, as the bars on the right show. The second ingredient is the distribution of the humidity within each class. On both rainy and dry days the humidity is Gaussian with the same spread, but rainy days are centred at a higher humidity. These class-conditional densities, in the middle panel, say which humidities are typical of each label. Multiplying each class-conditional density by its prior gives the joint density of the humidity and that label, in the bottom panel. The dry curve now peaks twice as high as the rainy one, because dry days are twice as common. Comparing the two joint densities at a humidity is enough to decide which label is more likely there. By Bayes' rule, each posterior is its joint density divided by the density of the humidity, and that divisor is the same for both labels. Now evaluate both joint densities at seventy percent humidity, the dashed line in the bottom panel. The dry one is the larger, so the prediction is no rain. The next slide turns the joint densities into the probability of rain at every humidity, the posterior curve.

Normalize the joint densities to get the posterior

For any humidity $x$, the joint densities of the previous slide add up to $\htmlClass{hl-px}{p(x)=w_1(x)+w_0(x)}$, so

\[p(y=1\mid x)=\htmlClass{hl-norm}{\frac{w_1(x)}{w_1(x)+w_0(x)}} =\frac{\pi\,p(x\mid y=1)}{\pi\,p(x\mid y=1)+(1-\pi)\,p(x\mid y=0)}.\]

Dividing the numerator and the denominator by $w_1(x)$,

\[p(y=1\mid x)=\htmlClass{hl-div}{\frac{1}{1+\frac{w_0(x)}{w_1(x)}}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\ln(w_1(x)/w_0(x))}}=\sigma\Big(\htmlClass{hl-lr}{\ln\frac{w_1(x)}{w_0(x)}}\Big),\]

with the sigmoid $\htmlClass{hl-sig}{\sigma(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}}$.

As $x$ varies, $\sigma\big(\ln(w_1(x)/w_0(x))\big)$ traces the curve in step 4.

For the densities of the previous slide, $\pi=\frac13$ and the Gaussian constants are equal, so

\[\begin{aligned}\ln\frac{w_1(x)}{w_0(x)}&=-\ln2+\ln p(x\mid y=1)-\ln p(x\mid y=0)\\&=-\ln2-\tfrac1{200}(x-80)^2+\tfrac1{200}(x-60)^2\\&=\frac{x-70}{5}-\ln2.\end{aligned}\] Comparing the joint densities decided between the labels at one humidity. Normalizing them gives the probability of each label at every humidity. The two joint densities add up to the density of the humidity, so dividing the joint density of rain by their sum gives the probability of rain. This probability has a standard form. Divide the top and the bottom by the joint density of rain. What remains depends on the humidity only through the log of the ratio of the two joint densities, and the expression is the sigmoid function of that log ratio. The same form returns for LDA and becomes the model itself in logistic regression. As the humidity varies, the sigmoid of the log ratio traces the posterior curve in step four. It rises with the humidity and crosses one half where the two joint densities cross in step three. For the two Gaussians of this example, the log ratio is a linear function of the humidity, as the Derivation drawer shows.

With $\bx\in\bbR^d$, each class has a Gaussian density $\bX\mid Y=j\sim\cN(\bmu_j,\Sigma_j)$ and prior probability $p(y=j)=\pi_j$.

LDA: Gaussian classes with a shared covariance

Suppose $\Sigma_j=\Sigma$ for every class. By Bayes' rule, with the joint density $w_j(\bx)=\pi_j\,p(\bx\mid y=j)$ of slide 7,

\[\ln p(y=j\mid\bx)=\htmlClass{hl-joint}{\ln w_j(\bx)}-\ln p(\bx)=\ln\pi_j+\ln p(\bx\mid y=j)-\ln p(\bx).\]

For the Gaussian, $\ln p(\bx\mid y=j)=-\tfrac12(\bx-\bmu_j)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_j)$ plus a normalizing term that depends only on $\Sigma$. Dropping that term and $-\ln p(\bx)$, which are the same for every class ($\doteq$),

\[\ln p(y=j\mid\bx)\doteq\ln\pi_j-\tfrac12\htmlClass{hl-maha}{(\bx-\bmu_j)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_j)}.\]

The quadratic form $(\bx-\bmu_j)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_j)$ is the squared Mahalanobis distance from $\bx$ to $\bmu_j$ under $\Sigma$.

Expand the quadratic form:

\[\ln p(y=j\mid\bx)\doteq\htmlClass{hl-quad}{-\tfrac12\bx^T\Sigma^{-1}\bx}+\htmlClass{hl-lin}{\bmu_j^T\Sigma^{-1}\bx}+\htmlClass{hl-const}{\ln\pi_j-\tfrac12\bmu_j^T\Sigma^{-1}\bmu_j}.\]

The quadratic term $\bx^T\Sigma^{-1}\bx$ is the same for every class and cancels when log-posteriors are compared. What remains is linear in $\bx$.

Linear discriminant analysis, LDA, assumes every class has the same covariance matrix. Taking logs in Bayes' rule, the log posterior of a class is the log of its joint density minus the log density of the input. The Gaussian's normalizing term and the log density of the input are the same for every class, and dropping them leaves the log prior minus half the squared Mahalanobis distance from the input to that class's mean. Sigma inverse weights that distance according to the shared covariance. Expand that squared Mahalanobis distance. Each log posterior then has a term quadratic in the input, a term linear in the input, and a constant term. Because the covariance is shared, the quadratic term is identical for every class. When the log posteriors of two classes are compared, that shared quadratic term cancels. The remaining difference is linear in the input. The next slide turns this difference into the classification rule and its straight boundary.

LDA: the decision boundary

For two classes, subtracting the log-posteriors of slide 10 gives

\[\ln p(y=1\mid\bx)-\ln p(y=0\mid\bx)=\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma,\] \[\begin{aligned}\bbeta^T&=\htmlClass{hl-beta}{(\muOne-\muZero)^T\Sigma^{-1}},\\ \cgamma&=\htmlClass{hl-gamma}{\ln\frac{\piOne}{\piZero}-\tfrac12(\muOne-\muZero)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\muOne+\muZero)}.\end{aligned}\]

Figure: $\Sigma_{11}=\Sigma_{22}=1$ and $\Sigma_{12}=\Sigma_{21}=0.4$.

Compare $p(y=1\mid\bx)$ and $p(y=0\mid\bx)$: \[\hat y(\bx)=\htmlClass{hl-rule}{\begin{cases}1,&\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma>0,\\0,&\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma<0.\end{cases}}\] Classes tie on $\htmlClass{hl-hyp}{\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma=0}$, a hyperplane (a line in the plane). For two classes, the difference of the two log posteriors is linear in the input, with coefficients beta and an offset gamma built from the means, the shared covariance and the class probabilities. On the right, both classes have the same tilted spread because their shared covariance has nonzero off-diagonal entries. The boundary between the classes is where the two log posteriors are equal, so where the linear function is zero: a line in this plane. With equal priors it passes through the midpoint between the two means. Its normal is sigma inverse times their difference, so with correlated features the line need not be perpendicular to the segment joining the means. In any number of dimensions, the boundary is the set where one linear function of the input is zero, a hyperplane: a line for two features, a plane for three.

LDA's posterior is logistic

The log-posteriors differ by $\htmlClass{hl-lin}{\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma}$, so $p(y=0\mid\bx)/p(y=1\mid\bx)=e^{-(\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma)}$, and the two posteriors add up to 1:

\[p(y=1\mid\bx)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma)}}=\htmlClass{hl-sig}{\sigma(\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma)}.\]

Each probability level is a parallel hyperplane $\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma=\text{constant}$; the figure marks levels 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75.

Direction: $\bbeta$ is normal to the boundary and points toward the fastest increase in $p(y=1\mid\bx)$.

Position: for fixed $\bbeta$, $\htmlClass{hl-gam}{\cgamma}$ shifts the boundary. Its signed distance from the origin along $\bbeta$ is $\htmlClass{hl-dist}{-\cgamma/\|\bbeta\|}$.

 LDA gives more than a boundary: it gives the probability of each class. The difference of the two log posteriors is the linear function of the previous slide, and the two posteriors add up to one. Together these make the posterior the sigmoid of that linear function. On the right, the shading shows the probability of class one, and the dashed lines, its level lines, are the inputs where it equals one quarter and three quarters; they are parallel to the boundary. The vector beta points perpendicular to each probability level line, toward increasing probability of class one. Its direction determines which way the probability rises, and its length controls how quickly it changes across the boundary. For a fixed beta, gamma shifts every level line together. The boundary is where beta dot x plus gamma equals zero; its signed distance from the origin along beta is minus gamma divided by the length of beta.

QDA, one covariance per class

With one $\htmlClass{hl-sigj}{\Sigma_j}$ per class the quadratic term stays, and the boundary $\ln p(y=1\mid\bx)=\ln p(y=0\mid\bx)$ is of the form

\[\htmlClass{hl-bnd}{\htmlClass{hl-xax}{\bx^TA\bx}+\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma=0},\]

a curve in the plane: quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA).

Alternatively, add the quadratic features $\bphi(\bx)=(x_1,\ldots,x_d,\ \htmlClass{hl-prod}{x_ix_j\text{ for }1\le i\le j\le d})$. A linear rule in $\htmlClass{hl-phi}{\bphi(\bx)}$ has the same form as LDA's rule and gives a quadratic boundary in $\bx$, without fitting a QDA density model.

For general Gaussian covariances, Bayes' rule gives $\ln p(y=j\mid\bx)\doteq\ln\pi_j-\tfrac12\ln|\Sigma_j|-\tfrac12(\bx-\bmu_j)^T\Sigma_j^{-1}(\bx-\bmu_j)$.

Here $|\Sigma_1|=|\Sigma_0|=0.84$ and $\piOne=\piZero=\frac12$, so the log-determinant and prior terms cancel:

\[\begin{aligned}A&=-\tfrac12\big(\Sigma_1^{-1}-\Sigma_0^{-1}\big),\\ \bbeta&=\Sigma_1^{-1}\muOne-\Sigma_0^{-1}\muZero,\\ \cgamma&=-\tfrac12\muOne^T\Sigma_1^{-1}\muOne+\tfrac12\muZero^T\Sigma_0^{-1}\muZero.\end{aligned}\] Quadratic discriminant analysis, QDA, lets each class keep its own covariance matrix. Then the quadratic terms of the two log posteriors differ, and they no longer cancel. The boundary, where the two log posteriors are equal, is the set where a quadratic function of the input is zero: a curve. Both class ellipses are tilted by their nonzero covariance; class one is also stretched along humidity and squeezed along pressure, and the boundary bends around it. There is another way to get a quadratic boundary. Add the squares and the pairwise products of the input coordinates as features, then fit a linear rule in that expanded feature vector. The rule is linear in the new features but quadratic in the original input.

Maximum likelihood for LDA and naive Bayes

Let $n_j$ be the number of training examples in class $j$. Maximum likelihood gives the class fraction and within-class averages:

\[\htmlClass{hl-pi}{\hat\pi_j=\frac{n_j}{n}},\qquad \htmlClass{hl-mu}{\hat\bmu_j=\frac1{n_j}\sum_{i:y_i=j}\bx_i}.\]

Center observations at their class means. Chapter 4's Gaussian result gives the shared covariance estimate:

\[\hat\Sigma=\htmlClass{hl-avg}{\frac1n\sum_{j=0}^1\sum_{i:y_i=j}}\htmlClass{hl-outer}{(\bx_i-\hat\bmu_j)(\bx_i-\hat\bmu_j)^T}.\]
Full LDA
Use every entry of $\hat\Sigma$: $d(d+1)/2$ covariance parameters.
Naive Bayes with shared variances
Keep only the diagonal of $\hat\Sigma$. Equivalently, $\hat D_{kk}=\frac1n\sum_i(x_{ik}-\hat\mu_{y_i,k})^2$: the pooled within-class sample variance of feature $k$ (MLE; $d$ parameters).
So far, we have assumed the distribution is given. In reality, we must learn it from data, for which we will use MLE. We fit LDA from labelled examples. The maximum-likelihood class probability is the fraction of training examples in that class, and each class mean is the average of its examples. To estimate one shared covariance, center each observation at its own class mean, form its outer product, and average those products over all n observations. Naive Bayes with shared variances keeps only the diagonal of this estimate, one pooled variance per feature.

Full LDA vs naive Bayes

Both Gaussian classes share a covariance. Naive Bayes ignores correlations between features.

Full LDA: fit the pooled covariance $\hat\Sigma$.

Naive Bayes: use $\hat D$ with $\hat D_{kk}=(\hat\Sigma)_{kk}$ and zero off-diagonal entries, so

\[p(\bx\mid y=j)=\htmlClass{hl-prod}{\prod_{k=1}^d p(x_k\mid y=j)}.\]

Both boundaries are lines. LDA fits $d(d+1)/2$ covariance entries; this shared-variance naive Bayes model fits $d$.

Both fits use the same class means and the same training points.

Full LDA keeps the correlations between input features in the shared covariance matrix. This Gaussian naive Bayes model keeps the same class means and the same individual feature variances, but sets the off-diagonal covariances to zero. That makes the features independent within each class. Both models have straight decision boundaries, yet the fitted lines differ on the same training points. Naive Bayes estimates fewer covariance parameters, while full LDA can use the correlations when the data support them.

Exponential-family class-conditionals

With Gaussian class-conditionals that share a covariance, the posterior is the sigmoid of a linear function of $\bx$: \[\htmlClass{hl-gs}{p(y=1\mid\bx)=\sigma(\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma)}\]

Is this form limited to Gaussian class-conditionals?

No! It holds for the exponential family with the same base term $f(\bx)$:

\[\htmlClass{hl-ef}{p(\bx\mid y=j)=\exp\big(\boldsymbol\eta_j^T\bx+\htmlClass{hl-f}{f(\bx)}+g_j\big)}.\]
\[\ln\frac{p(y=1\mid\bx)}{p(y=0\mid\bx)}=\htmlClass{hl-lin}{(\boldsymbol\eta_1-\boldsymbol\eta_0)^T\bx+\ln\frac{\piOne}{\piZero}+g_1-g_0}.\]
\[p(y=1\mid\bx)=\sigma\Big(\htmlClass{hl-eb}{\underbrace{(\boldsymbol\eta_1-\boldsymbol\eta_0)^T}_{\bbeta^T}}\bx+\htmlClass{hl-eg}{\underbrace{\ln\frac{\piOne}{\piZero}+g_1-g_0}_{\cgamma}}\Big).\]

If the posterior form is the same, why not model that posterior directly?

 The first part followed the generative route. With Gaussian class-conditionals that share a covariance, the posterior probability of class one is the sigmoid of a linear function of the input, with coefficients beta and offset gamma. On the right is the one-input example of slide eight: the two joint densities underneath, faint and scaled, and the posterior that Bayes' rule makes of them. Is this form limited to Gaussian class-conditionals? No. It holds for any exponential-family class-conditional whose statistic is the input itself, as long as every class has the same base term f of x. Each class has its own natural parameter, eta j, and its own normalizing term, g j. By Bayes' rule, the log ratio of the posteriors is the log ratio of the class probabilities plus the log ratio of the class-conditionals. The base term cancels, and what remains is linear in the input. So the posterior is again a sigmoid of a linear function. Beta is the difference of the natural parameters, and gamma is the log ratio of the class probabilities plus the difference of the g terms. The posterior has the same form for all of these class-conditionals. So should we model that posterior directly, without the class densities? Think about it before going on.

The two routes estimate different things. The generative way, LDA, estimates the class probability, both means and the shared covariance, and then computes beta and gamma from them. The discriminative way, logistic regression, skips the class densities and estimates beta and gamma directly. The two differ in size. The covariance has a number of free entries that grows with the square of the dimension, while logistic regression needs one coefficient per input dimension plus the offset, so the gap widens quickly as the dimension grows. The posterior, and so the decision, uses the parameters only through beta and gamma.

The sigmoid function

\[\sigma(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}\]

Its values lie in $\htmlClass{hl-range}{(0,1)}$: $\htmlClass{hl-pos}{e^{-u}>0}$, so the denominator exceeds 1.

$\sigma(0)=\htmlClass{hl-half}{\frac{1}{1+1}}=\frac12$.

$\htmlClass{hl-sym}{\sigma(-u)=1-\sigma(u)}$: for example, $\sigma(2)=0.881$ and $\sigma(-2)=0.119$.

It is near 0 below $u=-4$ and near 1 above $u=4$.

The symmetry: multiply the numerator and the denominator of $\sigma(-u)$ by $e^{-u}$,

\[\sigma(-u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{u}}=\frac{e^{-u}}{e^{-u}+1}=1-\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}}=1-\sigma(u).\]

The sigmoid increases in $u$: its derivative is $\sigma'(u)=\dfrac{e^{-u}}{(1+e^{-u})^2}>0$.

 The sigmoid appeared on both routes, so it is worth knowing well. It takes any real number, which we will call a score, and returns a number that grows with the score. On the right is its graph. The exponential is always positive, so the denominator is always greater than one: every value of the sigmoid lies strictly between zero and one, never reaching either. A score of zero lands exactly in the middle, because the exponential of zero is one. The curve is symmetric about that middle point: flipping the sign of a score turns its value into one minus that value, so a score and its negative give probabilities that add up to one. The two marked points show this for one pair of scores; the Derivation drawer has the algebra. Far from zero in either direction, the curve flattens, close to zero on the left and close to one on the right. So the sigmoid turns any real score into a probability: large negative scores mean near certainty of class zero, and large positive ones near certainty of class one.

The logistic model

\[p_{\btheta}(y=1\mid\bx)=\htmlClass{hl-model}{\sigma(\bbeta^T\bx+\cgamma)},\qquad\htmlClass{hl-theta}{\btheta=(\bbeta,\cgamma)},\]

With one input $x$, $p_{\btheta}(y=1\mid x)=\frac12$ at the midpoint $\htmlClass{hl-midx}{x=-\cgamma/\cbeta}$.

Example: $\cbeta=1$ and $\cgamma=-1$, so the midpoint is $x=1$.

Question: Double both $\cbeta$ and $\cgamma$: what changes?

The midpoint stays at 1, and the curve is steeper.

This cost has a name from chapter one. Put all of a distribution's mass on the observed label and compare it with the model's Bernoulli distribution: their cross-entropy is exactly this term. A rainy day then costs almost nothing, while a dry day, which the model thought unlikely, costs many times as much.

Cross-entropy and relative entropy

With $p_i$ and $q_i$ as on slide 31, let $\hat p_i$ be the distribution of the labels among all the examples with input $\bx_i$. Then

\[\begin{aligned}\frac1n\cL(\btheta)&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n H(p_i,q_i)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\htmlClass{hl-ce}{H(\htmlClass{dk1}{\hat p_i},q_i)}\\&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\htmlClass{hl-h}{\cml{H(\hat p_i)}}+\htmlClass{hl-d}{\cro{D(\hat p_i\,\|\,q_i)}}\Big).\end{aligned}\]

$H(\hat p_i)$ does not depend on $\btheta$, so minimizing the loss minimizes the average relative entropy $\frac1n\sum_i D(\hat p_i\|q_i)$, which is never negative (Sec. 1.4). At $\btheta=(\ln 3/10,-7\ln 3)$ the model gives 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, the observed fractions, so every $D$ is 0. This $\btheta$ minimizes $\cL$, so it is the ML estimate $\hat\btheta$.

Suppose $n_{\bx}$ examples have input $\bx$. They share the model's distribution $q$, and their label distributions $p_i$ average to $\htmlClass{dk1}{\hat p}$, the fraction of each label among them, so $\hat p_i=\hat p$ for each of them. Since $H(p,q)=\sum_y p(y)\ln\frac{1}{q(y)}$ is linear in $p$,

\[\sum_{i:\,\bx_i=\bx}H(p_i,q)=n_{\bx}\,H(\htmlClass{dk1}{\hat p},q)=\sum_{i:\,\bx_i=\bx}H(\htmlClass{dk1}{\hat p_i},q).\]

Summing over the distinct inputs gives the second equality.

Days with the same humidity share the model's distribution, so each day's label can be replaced by the observed label frequencies at its humidity without changing the total; the Derivation drawer shows why. The cross-entropy with the observed frequencies is the entropy of the observed labels plus the relative entropy from them to the model. Softmax regression

$K$ classes, $y\in\{1,\dots,K\}$. Each class $k$ has a score $\htmlClass{hl-z}{z_k=\bbeta_k^T\bx+\cgamma_k}$, called its logit, and

\[p(y=k\mid\bx)=\frac{\htmlClass{hl-num}{e^{z_k}}}{\htmlClass{hl-den}{\sum_{j=1}^Ke^{z_j}}},\]

So $\ln p(y=k\mid\bx)=z_k-\ln\sum_je^{z_j}$: a logit is a log-probability up to a constant shared by all classes, and $z_k-z_j=\ln\frac{p(y=k\mid\bx)}{p(y=j\mid\bx)}$.

Adding the same constant to every logit leaves $p$ unchanged.

A logit is not the log-odds of its class: it is the class's log-probability up to a constant shared by all classes, and only the difference of two logits is a log-odds. The softmax turns the logits into probabilities: exponentiate each one, so that every value is positive, and divide by the total, so that they add up to one. With two classes, dividing the numerator and the denominator by the first exponential leaves the sigmoid of the difference of the two logits, so that difference is the log-odds of slide twenty-four. Softmax regression with two classes is logistic regression, whose coefficients are the difference of the two classes' coefficients.

Next-token prediction as classification

A language model predicts the next token $w_t$ from the context $w_{1:t-1}$: a classification with $\htmlClass{hl-kv}{K=V}$ classes, one per token of the vocabulary $\cV$.

\[p(w_t=k\mid w_{1:t-1})=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j\in\cV}e^{z_j}},\] \[\htmlClass{hl-z}{\bz=\mW\bphi(w_{1:t-1})+\bb},\]

with one row of $\mW$ and one entry of $\bb$ per token.

With $V=10{,}000$ and $m=1{,}000$ features, $\mW$ and $\bb$ hold $\htmlClass{hl-count}{10{,}000\times1{,}001=10{,}010{,}000}$ parameters.

 A language model predicts each token from the tokens before it, and each prediction is a classification with one class per token of the vocabulary. In a neural language model, a network, with its embeddings and layers, first turns the context into a vector of features, as we will see later in the course. The last layer of a language model

With $\bphi$ fixed, fitting $\mW$ and $\bb$ by maximum likelihood is softmax regression with inputs $\bphi$: slide 36's loss and gradient with $\htmlClass{hl-phit}{\tbphi=(\bphi,1)}$ in place of $\tbx$,

\[\htmlClass{hl-grad}{\nabla_{(\mW_k,\,b_k)}\cL=-\sum_t\big(I(w_t=k)-p(k\mid w_{1:t-1})\big)\,\tbphi_t},\]

with $\mW_k$ the row of token $k$ and $\tbphi_t=\tbphi(w_{1:t-1})$.

Its last layer is softmax regression on features the network learns. Suppose the features were fixed. Then fitting the last layer is exactly softmax regression, with the feature vector, extended by a one, in place of the input: the same loss, and the same gradient, error times input, now summed over the positions of a text. In a real model the features are not fixed. The network learns them together with the last layer, by gradient descent on this same cross-entropy loss. So the output of a language model is built from what this lecture covered: logits that are linear in the features, a softmax, and a cross-entropy loss. What the rest of the network contributes is the features themselves.

Perplexity

A language model's training loss is the cross-entropy averaged over the positions of a text (slide 32).

Over a text of $T$ tokens, the average loss, measured in bits per token, is

\[\htmlClass{hl-bits}{\bar\ell=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\log_2\frac{1}{p(w_t\mid w_{1:t-1})}},\]

and the perplexity $\htmlClass{hl-ppl}{2^{\bar\ell}}$ is the number of equally likely tokens with the same cross-entropy.

A model uniform over $\htmlClass{hl-v}{V=10{,}000}$ tokens has perplexity 10,000. A model that gives each observed token probability $1/4$ spends 2 bits per token and has perplexity 4, as the exercise's model does for snow.

A language model is trained with the same loss as the rain model. Averaged over the positions of a text, it is a cross-entropy, so minimizing it minimizes the relative entropy from the text's next-token distributions to the model's, exactly as on the rain days. On the right is the model of the softmax exercise, for the word after the phrase tomorrow it will. With base-two logarithms, the average loss is measured in bits per token. Raising two to this average gives the perplexity: the number of equally likely tokens that would cost the same. If the next word is snow, this model pays the cost marked under its bars. A model that spreads its probability evenly over the vocabulary has a perplexity equal to the vocabulary size. The exercise's model pays as much for snow as a uniform choice among the tokens on the right, so its perplexity on that word is the number of those tokens. Lower perplexity means that the model is, on average, choosing among fewer tokens.

 $T$ (log scale) $T=$ When a language model generates text, it samples each next token from its predicted probabilities, and a common adjustment is to divide the logits by a temperature first. At temperature one nothing changes. Before going on, predict: does a lower temperature spread the probability more evenly, or concentrate it? Dividing by a temperature below one stretches the differences between the logits, so the largest one gains: the distribution sharpens. In the limit, all the probability goes to the token with the largest logit, and sampling always picks it. A temperature above one shrinks the differences, and the distribution flattens toward uniform. Dividing by a positive number never reorders the logits, so the ranking of the tokens is the same at every temperature; only how strongly the model prefers its first choice changes.

Summary

Generative classifiers model $p(\bx\mid y)$ and $p(y)$; LDA and naive Bayes fit class means and different forms of shared covariance.
Exponential-family class-conditionals can yield linear log-odds; logistic regression models that posterior directly.
Conditional maximum likelihood gives the cross-entropy loss. Gradient descent fits it, faster on a centered input.
Softmax regression handles $K$ classes, and a language model's last layer is softmax regression with $K=V$.
