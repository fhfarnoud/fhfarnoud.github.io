Softmax regression on learned features

We have seen count-based Markov models for predicting the next word hit the complexity wall quickly.

A language model instead computes a feature vector $\bphi(w_{1:t-1})$ based on previous words $w_{1:t-1}$ using a neural network and at the end uses softmax regression on it to produce the distribution of the next word $w_t$.

Question: How does a network compute features, and how are they learned?

Answering it needs:

  • 2a way to compute gradients through them;

 A language model predicts the next token with softmax regression, as we saw at the end of the linear classification lecture. The figure shows where that last layer gets its input: a neural network turns the context into a feature vector. So the question is how a network computes those features, and how it learns them from text. Answering it takes three things, one for each part of this lecture. First, functions that build their own features from the input. Second, a way to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to weights buried deep inside such a function. Third, training that gives a network that works on new data, not only on the examples it was fitted to. Each of these rests on a tool from an earlier chapter. The losses are negative log-likelihoods, the gradients come from the chain rule, a good starting point comes from the variance of a sum, and regularization is a prior. Feature engineering

A simple model can only use the representation it is given. Feature engineering chooses features $\bphi(\bx)$ by hand so that it works.

Example: one-second waves $\bx\in\bbR^{64}$ at 2 cycles per second (class 0) or 3 (class 1), random phase, noise standard deviation 0.7; 200 training waves per class.

Logistic regression on two raw samples, $(x_{17},x_{30})$: 53.5% right on its training waves, 48.2% on 2,000 new ones.

The same model on $\bphi(\bx)=(|X_2|,|X_3|)$, the Fourier amplitudes at 2 and 3 cycles per second: 100% on training and new waves. Its line lies close to $|X_3|=|X_2|$.

Even all 64 raw samples give only 66% on training and 52% on new waves: a wave and its negative are equally likely, so both classes average to zero.

The Fourier amplitude keeps the frequency and ignores the phase, the nuisance here.

With the samples $x_k$ at times $t_k=k/64$, $k=0,\dots,63$, the amplitude at $f$ cycles per second is

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{|X_f|=\frac{2}{64}\,\Big|\sum_{k=0}^{63}x_k\,e^{-2\pi i f t_k}\Big|},\]

which is about 1 for a wave of frequency $f$ and amplitude 1, whatever its phase, and near 0 for a wave of the other frequency (the noise adds a little).

A wave $\sin(2\pi ft+\varphi)$ with $\varphi$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$ has mean $\E[\sin(2\pi ft+\varphi)]=0$ at every $t$, in both classes. A linear score $\bw^T\bx+b$ then has the same mean in both classes; only its spread can differ, and a single threshold on it barely uses that.

 Before networks learned their own features, people chose them by hand. A simple model such as logistic regression can only use the representation it is given; feature engineering picks a representation in which the simple model works. In the example, each input is one second of a noisy wave, sampled sixty-four times. Class zero oscillates twice per second and class one three times, and each wave starts at a random point of its cycle. The panels show two waves of each class. First, fit logistic regression to two of the raw samples of each wave. The left panel shows these two samples for a hundred training waves of each class, and the solid line is where the fitted model gives probability one half. The classes form one cloud, and the line splits it about evenly: on new waves the model does no better than a coin. Now fit the same model to two other numbers computed from each wave: the amplitude of its component at two cycles per second, and at three. In the right panel the classes sit in opposite corners, and the fitted line, which runs close to the diagonal where the two amplitudes are equal, separates them: the model is right on every training wave and every new one. Only the representation changed. Giving the model all sixty-four raw samples does not rescue it. With a random phase, a wave and its negative are equally likely, so every sample averages to zero in both classes, and no weighted sum of the samples tells the classes apart. The amplitude keeps the frequency, which separates the classes, and discards the phase, which only varies from wave to wave. Choosing it took knowing what the classes have in common. The rest of this lecture is about learning features from the data instead.

Hand-designed features, and learned ones

Above the axis, hand-designed features; below it, learned ones (to scale). Fields: speech, images, text; no icon: a general method.

Why now (around 2010)

Data: ImageNet (2009); its challenge set has 1.2 million labelled images in 1000 classes.

Compute: GPUs (2009, Raina et al.); AlexNet (2012), “between five and six days to train on two GTX 580 3GB GPUs”.

Training methods: ReLU (2010), dropout (2012).

ILSVRC-2012, sorting photographs into 1000 categories: an ensemble of convolutional networks reached 15.3% top-5 error against 26.2% for the second-best entry, which averaged classifiers on Fisher vectors of hand-designed, densely sampled features.
For decades, features were designed by hand, a separate design for each kind of data; the small icons mark the field of each item. In speech, mel-frequency cepstral coefficients came out best among the representations Davis and Mermelstein compared in 1980. In vision, Lowe's SIFT, first published in 1999 and in full in 2004, describes image patches in a way that is invariant to image scale and rotation, and HOG describes them by histograms of gradient directions, fed to a linear support vector machine. For text, the features were counts of words and n-grams, as in the language models of Section 1.3. Learned features are almost as old. In 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed that with backpropagation, internal hidden units which are not part of the input or output come to represent important features of the task domain. Convolutional networks then read handwritten digits directly from pixels. In 2009 graphics processors made training deep networks far faster, in 2010 Nair and Hinton showed that rectified linear units improve such networks, though the rectifier itself is older, and in 2012 AlexNet put the pieces together. Bengio, Courville and Vincent's survey puts the cost of the old approach plainly: designing features by hand is laborious, and it was needed because learning algorithms could not yet find the discriminative information themselves. LeCun and coauthors had made the same case in 1998: hand-crafted feature extraction can be advantageously replaced by carefully designed learning machines that operate directly on pixel images. So learned features were not new in 2012; what changed around 2010 was data and compute. ImageNet, published in 2009, supplied over a million labelled images; graphics processors trained AlexNet in under a week; ReLU units sped up training and dropout, introduced in 2012, reduced overfitting. Sutton later drew the general lesson, that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin; the third lecture of this part returns to it as scaling laws. The result was decisive in image classification. In the 2012 ImageNet challenge, which sorts photographs into a thousand categories, an ensemble of convolutional networks made far fewer top-five errors, predictions whose five highest-ranked categories miss the true one, than the second-best entry, which was built on hand-designed features.

Learning the transformation: linear layers

We saw hand-designed features enabled classification. Can we make this automatic instead?

Idea: Add a layer that learns the features, then combine them via another. The simplest choice is linear for both:

\[\bh=\bA\bx+\bc,\qquad\hat y=\bw^T\bh+d.\]

Substituting,

\[\hat y=\bw^T(\bA\bx+\bc)+d=(\bw^T\bA)\bx+(\bw^T\bc+d),\]

a linear function of $\bx$, for any number of hidden units.

Any number of layers: $\bW_3(\bW_2(\bW_1\bx))=(\bW_3\bW_2\bW_1)\bx$, one matrix.

Without a nonlinearity, a deep network computes the same functions as a single linear layer. Hand-picked features worked, but we want the network to learn them. The simplest learnable features are linear: each hidden unit is a weighted sum of the inputs plus an offset, which together form a matrix A and a vector c. Substituting the hidden layer into the output gives one weight vector times the input plus one offset. The expanded and the compact views on the right are the same network, and both compute a linear function of the input. More layers do not help either. A linear layer applied to the output of a linear layer is again linear, because the product of the weight matrices is a single matrix. So stacking linear layers collapses into one linear model, however wide or deep the stack. Something nonlinear has to sit between the layers.

The fix: an activation function

A neuron applies a nonlinear activation $g$ to its weighted sum:

\[z=\bw^T\bx+b,\qquad a=g(z).\]

With $g$ between the layers, $\hat y=\bw^Tg(\bA\bx+\bc)+d$ no longer collapses. ReLU is a hinge: zero, then a straight line.

Practice: $\bx=(2,-1)$, $\bw=(3,2)$, $b=-4$. Find $z$, $\relu(z)$ and $\sigma(z)$.

$z=6-2-4=0$, so $\relu(z)=0$ and $\sigma(z)=0.5$.

 The fix is a nonlinear function applied to each unit's weighted sum. A neuron computes the weighted sum z and passes it through an activation function g. With g between the layers, the output can no longer be rewritten as one linear function of the input. The two common choices are below the neuron: ReLU, zero for negative z and the identity for positive z, which is a hinge; and the sigmoid, which squashes z into the interval between zero and one. Compute the output of one neuron with these inputs, weights and offset, first with ReLU and then with the sigmoid, before going on. The weighted sum lands exactly on the corner of the hinge, where ReLU is zero. The sigmoid there is at its midpoint, halfway between zero and one.

The multilayer perceptron

$\bh^{(0)}=\bx$, and for each layer $l=1,\dots,L$, \[\bh^{(l)}=g\big(\bW^{(l)}\bh^{(l-1)}+\bb^{(l)}\big).\]

The superscript $(l)$ labels the layer; it is not a power. Layers are counted without the input: the network on the right has 3.

Layer $l$ has $n_{\text{out}}(n_{\text{in}}+1)$ parameters, a weight per connection and an offset per unit. A 3-4-2 network has $4\cdot4+2\cdot5=26$.

 Stacking such layers gives the multilayer perceptron. Each layer multiplies the previous layer's vector by a weight matrix, adds an offset vector, and applies the activation to each entry. The superscript in parentheses says which layer a quantity belongs to. In the network on the right every unit feeds every unit of the next layer, and the layers counted are the two hidden layers and the output layer; the input is not counted. Each layer has one weight for every connection and one offset for every unit it computes. So a layer with n in inputs and n out units has n out times n in plus one parameters. For three inputs, four hidden units and two outputs, the two layers' counts add up to the total shown.

Many hinges make any curve

Let's see how ReLU can produce non-linearity in 1D.

Each ReLU unit adds a hinge:

\[\hat y(x)=d+\sum_{j=1}^k v_j\,\relu\big(x-t_j\big).\]

With the hinges fixed at $t_j=\frac{j-1}k$, least squares fits $d$ and the $v_j$ to a smooth bump.

Each unit contributes a hinge: zero up to a point, then a straight line. The output adds the hinges with weights, so the network computes a function made of straight pieces. On the right, the dashed curve is a smooth bump, and the green curve is the best fit by least squares with one unit; the ticks at the bottom mark where the hinges bend. This is the universal approximation theorem: with enough units, one hidden layer approximates any continuous function on a bounded set as closely as we want. Output layers, distributions and losses

The hidden layers build the features; the output layer and the loss decide what the network predicts and how it is scored.

The output layer computes the parameters of a distribution for $y$ given $\bx$; the loss is that distribution's negative log-likelihood, so training is maximum likelihood.

Here $z$ (or $\bz$) is the output layer's linear function of the last hidden layer $\bh^{(L)}$.

Its last layer is softmax regression with inputs $\bh^{(L)}$; the hidden layers compute those features, and maximum likelihood fits both. What a network outputs, and how it is scored, depends on the task, and each choice comes from a distribution we have met. The output layer computes the parameters of a distribution for the label given the input, and the loss is that distribution's negative log-likelihood. For regression the output is the mean of a Gaussian, whose negative log-likelihood is, up to scale and a constant, the squared error of least squares. For two classes, a sigmoid output is the probability of class one in a Bernoulli distribution, and its negative log-likelihood is the cross-entropy of logistic regression. For K classes, a softmax output gives the probabilities of a categorical distribution, with the same loss. So a network classifier is softmax regression whose inputs are the last hidden layer's values. The hidden layers compute the features and the last layer classifies with them; training fits both at once, by maximum likelihood.

Partial derivatives

Backpropagation starts from one question: how much does a small change in an input change the output?

Nudge the inputs of $z=f(u,v)$:

\[\Delta z\approx\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\Delta u+\frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\Delta v.\]

A partial derivative, such as $\partial z/\partial u$, shows the impact of a small change in one input on the output, with the other inputs held fixed.

For $z=uv$ at $u=5$, $v=1$: $\partial z/\partial u=v=1$ and $\partial z/\partial v=u=5$, so $\Delta z\approx1\cdot\Delta u+5\cdot\Delta v$.

To raise $z$ fastest for a step of a given size, move each input in proportion to its partial derivative: $\Delta u=1\cdot\eta$, $\Delta v=5\cdot\eta$ give $\Delta z\approx26\,\eta$.

To lower a loss, flip the sign: $\Delta\theta=-\eta\,\partial\cL/\partial\theta$, gradient descent.

 Start with a function of two inputs. A partial derivative says how much a small change in one input moves the output while the other input stays fixed, so nudging both inputs changes the output by approximately the sum of each partial derivative times its nudge. In the diagram, a product of two inputs, the values flow forward above the edges. For a product, each input's partial derivative is the value of the other input. They appear below the edges in rose: here a change in v moves the output five times as much as the same change in u. Which nudge of a given size raises the output the most? The one that moves each input in proportion to its partial derivative, so the input with the larger partial derivative moves more. To lower a loss instead, move each parameter against its partial derivative. That is gradient descent, and what it needs is the partial derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter.

Building blocks 1 to 3

A network is built from a few simple operations, and each passes partial derivatives back by its own rule.

A network is built from a handful of simple operations, and each has a fixed rule for its local partial derivatives. Each row shows one block in general and on an example. Values flow forward above the edges, and backward messages travel back below them, starting from one at the output. A backward message is the partial derivative of the output, and later of the loss, with respect to the value on its edge. An addition passes the message unchanged to both of its inputs. A multiplication sends each input the value of the other input, as for the product on the previous slide. A negation flips the sign of the message.

Building blocks 4 to 6

Three more blocks, the nonlinear ones, complete the set a network needs.

Three more blocks. ReLU acts as a gate: when its input is positive it passes the message on unchanged, and when its input is negative or zero it blocks it and sends zero back. The sigmoid scales the message by its output times one minus its output. This scale is largest at an input of zero, where the output is one half, and shrinks toward zero as the input grows in either direction. Squaring scales the message by twice its input: the larger the input, the more a change in it moves the output. So far every message started as one at the output. If a block receives some other message c, every message it sends back is multiplied by c, because each change it causes in its output is felt c times over further on.

Chaining blocks

With the six blocks in hand, the next question is how blocks in a row combine their local partial derivatives.

Pass the message back through one block after another, multiplying by each block's local partial derivative.

Linear: $\dfrac{dz}{du}=\dfrac{dz}{dv}\dfrac{dv}{du}=2\cdot3=6$. Check: $z=6u$.

Nonlinear: $\dfrac{dz}{du}=(-1)(2u)=-6$ at $u=3$. Check: $z=-u^2$.

Multiplying the local partial derivatives along the path gives the derivative of the output with respect to the start of the path. Now combine blocks. Two multiplications by constants in a row: first by three, then by two. The message starts as one at the output. The second block multiplies the message by its own local partial derivative, so the message reaching v is twice the starting one. The first block multiplies it again, and the message reaching u is the product of the two local partial derivatives. Indeed the output is six times u. The same works for nonlinear blocks. Square u, then negate. The negation flips the sign of the message on its way to s. The squaring block then scales it by twice the value of u, which gives the derivative of minus u squared at this point. Multiplying local partial derivatives along the path is exactly the chain rule of calculus. Backpropagation is the chain rule, computed one block at a time from the output back toward the inputs.

Branching, and the backpropagation rule

Graphs also branch, and a block's message must travel back along every one of its inputs.

First $v=a+b$, then $z=uv$, with $u=5$, $a=3$, $b=1$: forward, $v=4$ and $z=20$.

At $\times$, the message 1 goes back to $u$ multiplied by $v$, giving 4, and to $v$ multiplied by $u$, giving 5.

At $+$ the message 5 passes to both $a$ and $b$. Check: $z=u(a+b)$, so $\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}=a+b=4$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial a}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial b}=u=5$.

Start with the message 1 at the output. At each block, multiply the incoming message by the block's local partial derivative and pass it to the block's inputs. A graph can branch. Here a sum feeds one input of a product, and the other input comes straight in. The forward pass computes the sum and then the product. Backward, the product sends its incoming message to each of its inputs, multiplied by the value of the other input. The sum passes its message unchanged to both of its inputs. The check by calculus agrees with every message. This gives the backpropagation rule. Start with a message of one at the output; at each block, multiply the incoming message by the block's local partial derivative and send it on to the block's inputs. The message that reaches each input is the derivative of the output with respect to it.

Backpropagation in the full network

The blocks and the two rules are all that the 2-2-1 network of slide 25 needs; here it is as a computation graph, with $\bx=(1,2)$, $y=1$, $\cL=(\hat y-y)^2$.

Here is the whole network of the example as a computation graph, built from the blocks we have met: products of weights and inputs, additions of offsets, the ReLU gates, the output, and the squared error against the label. The forward pass has filled in every value above the edges, ending with the loss. Backward, the message starts as one at the loss. The squaring block scales it by twice the error, and the additions pass it on to the output offset and to both products of the output layer. Each output weight's message is that message times its unit's hidden value, so the weight of the inactive unit gets nothing. The same products send messages to the hidden values: each gets the message times its output weight. Then the ReLU gates act. The first unit is on and passes its message through; the second is off and blocks it. Last, the first layer. The additions pass each unit's message to its offset and to its two products, and each product sends its weight the message times the input. The first unit's parameters get nonzero messages, and the second unit's get zero, because its gate is closed. These nine messages are the gradient.

The same computation in matrix form

For a whole layer at once, the same messages take a compact matrix form.

With $\bz^{(l)}=\bW^{(l)}\bh^{(l-1)}+\bb^{(l)}$ and $\bh^{(l)}=g(\bz^{(l)})$, let $\bdelta^{(l)}=\partial\cL/\partial\bz^{(l)}$, the messages at layer $l$'s pre-activations. Then

\[\begin{aligned}\htmlClass{dk1}{\bdelta^{(l)}}&\htmlClass{dk1}{{}=\big(\bW^{(l+1)T}\bdelta^{(l+1)}\big)\odot g'(\bz^{(l)})},\\ \htmlClass{dk2}{\nabla_{\bW^{(l)}}\cL}&\htmlClass{dk2}{{}=\bdelta^{(l)}\,\bh^{(l-1)T}},\qquad \nabla_{\bb^{(l)}}\cL=\bdelta^{(l)},\end{aligned}\]

where $\odot$ multiplies two vectors entry by entry, $(\mathbf a\odot\mathbf b)_j=a_jb_j$.

Error times input at every layer, as for logistic regression (32-3, slide 29). A softmax output with the cross-entropy has the output error $\htmlClass{dk3}{\bdelta^{(L)}=\bp-\be_y}$.

 Output layer $\hat y=\bw^T\bh^{(1)}+d$, $\bw=(v_1,v_2)$: \[\begin{aligned}\delta^{(2)}&=2(\hat y-y)=4,\\ \nabla_{\bw}\cL&=\delta^{(2)}\bh^{(1)}=4\,(3,0)=(12,0),\\ \bdelta^{(1)}&=(\bw\,\delta^{(2)})\odot g'(\bz^{(1)})\\&=(4,8)\odot(1,0)=(4,0),\\ \nabla_{\bW^{(1)}}\cL&=\bdelta^{(1)}\bx^T=\begin{pmatrix}4&8\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\\ \nabla_{\bb^{(1)}}\cL&=(4,0).\end{aligned}\]

Unit $i$ of layer $l$ feeds every unit $j$ of layer $l+1$ through $z^{(l+1)}_j=\sum_iW^{(l+1)}_{ji}h^{(l)}_i+b^{(l+1)}_j$. By the chain rule its value receives the sum of their messages,

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{\frac{\partial\cL}{\partial h^{(l)}_i}=\sum_j\delta^{(l+1)}_jW^{(l+1)}_{ji}=\big(\bW^{(l+1)T}\bdelta^{(l+1)}\big)_i,}\]

and the activation scales it by its slope: $\delta^{(l)}_i=\frac{\partial\cL}{\partial h^{(l)}_i}\,g'(z^{(l)}_i)$. For ReLU, $g'$ is 1 for a unit that is on and 0 for one that is off.

$W^{(l)}_{ij}$ enters only $z^{(l)}_i$, with coefficient $h^{(l-1)}_j$, so $\partial\cL/\partial W^{(l)}_{ij}=\delta^{(l)}_ih^{(l-1)}_j$, the entries of $\bdelta^{(l)}\bh^{(l-1)T}$; $b^{(l)}_i$ enters with coefficient 1.

For the softmax with the cross-entropy, $-\ln p_y=-z_y+\ln\sum_ke^{z_k}$, so $\partial(-\ln p_y)/\partial z_k=p_k-\mathbb 1[k=y]$ (32-3, slide 37).

 The block-by-block computation has a compact form for whole layers. Call delta the vector of messages arriving at a layer's pre-activations. One layer back, the messages are multiplied by the transposed weight matrix, which adds up the messages from every unit a hidden value feeds, and then, entry by entry, by the activation's slope. The elementwise product multiplies each unit's backward message by that unit's local derivative, which for ReLU passes it, a one, or blocks it, a zero: the ReLU block of slide twenty-eight applied to a whole layer. A layer's weight gradient is its delta times the layer's input, transposed, and its offset gradient is delta itself. This is the error times input form of logistic regression, now at every layer. With a softmax output and the cross-entropy loss, the output error is the vector of predicted probabilities minus the indicator of the observed class. The check uses the numbers of the full graph. The output error is twice the prediction error. Multiplying it by the output weights and the ReLU slopes gives the first layer's deltas, and their outer product with the input reproduces the nine messages of the graph.
Part 2 done: the map pulls back and enters training. That completes backpropagation: the chain rule, run backward one block at a time, gives every parameter's derivative, in the form error times input at every layer. The third part turns these gradients into training that works: the step size, minibatches, the starting weights, and the fixes for a network that memorizes its data.

One gradient step

With every partial derivative in hand, one gradient-descent step updates all nine parameters.

$\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\dfrac{\partial\cL}{\partial\theta}$ for every parameter $\theta$, step size $\eta$.

Slide 31's gradient, in the order $w_{11},w_{12},w_{21},w_{22},b_1,b_2,v_1,v_2,d$: $\nabla\cL=(4,8,0,0,4,0,12,0,4)$, and $\|\nabla\cL\|^2=256$.

To first order, $\Delta\cL\approx-\eta\|\nabla\cL\|^2$: $-2.56$ at $\eta=0.01$.

$\eta=0.01$: the loss goes from 4 to 1.93.

$\eta=0.1$: 2.31; the step overshoots.

$\eta=1$: 25. Both ReLUs are off, and only $d$ still receives a gradient.

To first order, the loss then changes by minus eta times the squared length of the gradient, which predicts a large drop for our example even at a small step. The training loop

Repeating the forward pass, backward pass and update over the data, batch by batch, is the training loop.

Batch: the $B$ examples used for one update. Iteration: one update. Epoch: one pass through all $n$ training examples, $n/B$ iterations.

Initialize $\btheta$ at random.
For each epoch: shuffle the data and split it into batches of $B$;
  for each batch: forward pass, loss, backward pass (the batch's average gradient), update $\btheta\leftarrow\btheta-\eta\nabla\cL_{\text{batch}}$.
Stop when the validation loss stops improving. Training repeats four steps: run the forward pass on a batch, compute the loss, backpropagate, and update the parameters. One update is an iteration, and the examples it uses form a batch. An epoch is one pass through all of the training data, so it consists of the number of examples divided by the batch size iterations. Minibatch gradient descent shuffles the data at every epoch and splits it into batches, and it stops once the loss on held-out validation data stops improving.

The minibatch gradient is an estimator

A minibatch gradient is computed from a random sample of examples, so Chapter 2's questions apply: is it unbiased, and how variable is it?

Draw $B$ examples uniformly, with replacement: $\hat\bg_B=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B\nabla\cL_{i_b}$.

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{\E[\hat\bg_B]=\nabla\cL},\qquad \htmlClass{dk2}{\Var(\hat g_{B,k})=\frac{\sigma_k^2}{B}},\]

with $\sigma_k^2$ the variance of coordinate $k$ over the examples: unbiased, with the variance of a mean (Ch. 2) of independent draws (Ch. 4).

$B=4$ halves the standard deviation,

and $B=16$ cuts it to 1/4. Stochastic gradient descent in 32-3 used $B=1$.

$\partial\cL/\partial d$ for the 2-2-1 network on 100 synthetic examples; 50 minibatches per row; shaded: the full gradient $\pm\sigma/\sqrt B$.

Each index $i_b$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,n\}$, so $\E[\nabla\cL_{i_b}]=\frac1n\sum_i\nabla\cL_i=\nabla\cL$, and the average of $B$ such terms has the same mean.

Draws with replacement are independent, so the variance of their sum is the sum of their variances (Ch. 4): $\Var\big(\sum_b\nabla_k\cL_{i_b}\big)=B\sigma_k^2$, where $\sigma_k^2=\frac1n\sum_i\big(\nabla_k\cL_i-\nabla_k\cL\big)^2$. Dividing the sum by $B$ divides the variance by $B^2$.

 A minibatch gradient is an estimate of the full-data gradient, and the estimators of Chapter 2 describe it. If the examples are drawn uniformly at random, each draw's gradient has the full gradient as its mean, so their average is unbiased. The figure uses the network of the backpropagation example on a hundred synthetic examples: the top row shows fifty single-example estimates of the derivative with respect to the output offset, and the vertical line is its full-data value. They scatter widely, and many have the wrong sign. Drawn with replacement, the gradients in a batch are independent, so the variance of their average is the per-example variance divided by the batch size: the variance of a sum, from Chapter 4. Batches of four halve the standard deviation. Batches of sixteen cut it to a quarter. Stochastic gradient descent in the linear classification lecture used batches of one; larger batches give steadier steps at a higher cost per step.

Initialization

Gradient descent needs a starting point, and the scale of the starting weights decides whether signals survive many layers.

Symmetry: units with equal weights compute equal values and receive equal gradients, so every update keeps them equal. Weights start random.

With independent weights of mean 0 and variance $\sigma^2$, independent of $\bh$ (Ch. 4, the variance of a sum):

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{\Var(z_j)=\Var\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nW_{ji}h_i\Big)=n\sigma^2\,\E[h_i^2]}.\]

A linear layer keeps the scale with $\sigma^2=1/n$. ReLU halves $\E[h^2]$, so ReLU layers need $\sigma^2=2/n$.

 $n\sigma^2$

Each term $W_{ji}h_i$ has mean 0 (the weight has mean 0 and is independent of $h_i$), so $\Var(W_{ji}h_i)=\E[W_{ji}^2]\,\E[h_i^2]=\sigma^2\E[h_i^2]$. The terms are uncorrelated, so their variances add: $n\sigma^2\E[h_i^2]$.

If $z$ is symmetric about 0, $\relu(z)^2$ equals $z^2$ on half of the outcomes and 0 on the other half, so $\E[\relu(z)^2]=\frac12\E[z^2]=\frac12\Var(z)$. Then $\Var(z^{(l+1)})=n\sigma^2\cdot\frac12\Var(z^{(l)})$, constant from layer to layer when $n\sigma^2=2$.

The figure: the plan's simulation, an input of 1,000 standard normal vectors in $\bbR^{100}$ and 10 ReLU layers of width 100 without offsets, one draw of the weights. Scaling every weight by $c$ scales layer $l$'s activations by $c^l$, so one draw gives every $n\sigma^2$.

 Where should gradient descent start? Not with equal weights: units with the same incoming weights compute the same value and receive the same gradient, so they would stay identical forever. So the weights start random, and the question is how large they should be. A pre-activation is a sum of n products of weights and inputs, so by the variance of a sum its variance is n times the weight variance times the mean square input. On the right, ten ReLU layers of width one hundred with n sigma squared equal to one: the activations shrink layer after layer. ReLU sets half of its inputs to zero, which halves the mean square. Doubling the weight variance makes up for it, and the activations keep their scale through all ten layers. Doubling the variance once more, the activations grow by the same factor at every layer and explode. Training a deep network starts from weights that keep the scale; the slider varies n sigma squared.

With enough parameters, a network can fit its training data exactly, noise included. The curves come from a regression on eight noisy XOR points, for a network with two hidden units and one with eight. The training loss keeps falling toward zero, while the loss on held-out validation points reaches a minimum and then rises: the network has started to fit the noise. This is the bias and variance trade-off of Chapter 5, for a network. More parameters let the fit follow the data more closely, which lowers the bias and raises the variance.

Three fixes

Three standard tools keep a network from fitting the noise.

Early stopping
Keep the weights with the best validation loss; stop after $k$ epochs without improvement (the patience).
Dropout
In training, each unit is kept with probability $1-p$: a mask $m\sim\ber(1-p)$, output $m\,h$, so $\E[m\,h]=(1-p)h$. At test time all units are kept, scaled by $1-p$.
Weight decay
Adding $\frac\lambda2\|\btheta\|^2$ to the negative log-likelihood is MAP with the prior $\btheta\sim\cN(\bzero,\lambda^{-1}\bI)$: ridge regression (Ch. 5) for a network.

With the prior $\btheta\sim\cN(\bzero,\lambda^{-1}\bI)$, $\ln p(\btheta)=-\frac\lambda2\|\btheta\|^2+\text{const}$, so by Bayes' rule

\[\htmlClass{dk1}{-\ln p(\btheta\mid\cD)=\underbrace{-\ln p(\cD\mid\btheta)}_{\text{the loss}}+\frac\lambda2\|\btheta\|^2+\text{const},}\]

and the minimizer of the penalized loss is the posterior mode, the MAP estimate (Ch. 3). For a linear model with Gaussian noise this is ridge regression (Ch. 5).

 Three fixes keep a network from memorizing its data. Early stopping watches the validation loss during training and keeps the weights from the epoch where it was lowest; training stops once it has not improved for a set number of epochs, the patience. Dropout sets each hidden unit to zero at random during training, independently at every step, so no unit can rely on another. A unit is kept with probability one minus p, so its expected output is scaled by that probability; at test time every unit is kept, and its output is scaled the same way so that the next layer sees what it saw in training. Weight decay adds a penalty on the squared length of the parameter vector to the negative log-likelihood. The sum is the negative log-posterior under a Gaussian prior with mean zero, so the fit is the MAP estimate: ridge regression from Chapter 5, applied to a network.

Calibration

Are a network's probabilities trustworthy? A classifier is calibrated if among its predictions made with confidence 0.8, about 80% are right, and so for every confidence.

Toy: 100 predictions at confidence 0.5, 45 right; 100 at 0.9, 60 right.

Expected calibration error, the gap averaged over the predictions:

\[\mathrm{ECE}=\tfrac{100}{200}\,|0.45-0.5|+\tfrac{100}{200}\,|0.6-0.9|=0.175.\] Are a network's probabilities trustworthy? A classifier is calibrated if its confidence matches its accuracy: among the predictions made with a given confidence, that fraction is right. In the reliability diagram on the right, each bar is the fraction of correct predictions in one group, placed at the group's confidence, and the dashed diagonal is perfect calibration. The toy classifier's low-confidence group is close to it. The high-confidence group is right far less often than it claims: the classifier is overconfident. The expected calibration error averages the gaps between confidence and accuracy, weighting each group by its number of predictions.

Maximum likelihood rewards calibration

Calibration follows from the loss the network is trained with.

\[\E_{Y\sim p}\Big[\ln\frac{1}{q(Y)}\Big]=H(p)+D(p\,\|\,q),\]

 Why would a network be calibrated at all? Maximum likelihood rewards it. If the label follows a distribution p and the model predicts q, the expected log loss is the entropy of p plus the relative entropy from p to q, as in the linear classification lecture. The entropy does not depend on q, and the relative entropy is zero only when q equals p. Summary

Features are learned by layers of weighted sums with nonlinear activations; the output layer gives a distribution's parameters, and the loss is its negative log-likelihood.
Backpropagation is the chain rule run backward: error times input at every layer.
Training is maximum likelihood with estimated gradients, a variance-preserving start and a prior.
Calibration is what maximum likelihood rewards.
